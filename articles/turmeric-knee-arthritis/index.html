Does turmeric help knee arthritis pain?

Turmeric (curcumin) is one of the most popular supplements for sore knees. A surgeon reads the meta-analyses of the randomised trials: what it did for pain, how it compared with anti-inflammatory painkillers, what dose, and what the trials can't tell you.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 19 November 2026

The verdict

“Turmeric (curcumin) supplements ease knee arthritis pain”

C · Early

Key points

  • A meta-analysis of 16 randomised trials (1,810 adults, up to 16 weeks) found turmeric extracts reduced knee pain and improved function compared with placebo — but the trials varied widely and had a moderate risk of bias.
  • In the same analysis, turmeric did about as well as anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs), with 12 % fewer side effects; only five trials made that comparison.
  • Higher doses did not relieve pain better than lower doses in a meta-analysis of 11 trials.
  • No trial showed that turmeric protects cartilage or slows arthritis, and no supplement in a review of 69 trials gave clinically important pain relief in the long term.
  • Turmeric is among the supplements named in liver-injury registries — tell your doctor if you take it.

Turmeric — or its main active ingredient, curcumin — is one of the supplements people with sore knees reach for most often. Unlike many joint supplements, it has been tested in quite a few randomised trials. So does it work?

The honest answer is more interesting than "yes" or "myth": it probably helps a little, for a few months, but the evidence is weaker than the headlines suggest.

What the trials found for pain

The most careful meta-analysis pooled 16 randomised trials with 1,810 adults with knee osteoarthritis, each lasting up to 16 weeks.[1]

  • Compared with placebo (11 trials), turmeric extracts reduced knee pain and improved physical function.[1]
  • But the results varied a lot from trial to trial, and the trials had a moderate risk of bias — the authors themselves wrote that this leaves "some uncertainty about the true effect".[1]
  • People with a higher BMI improved less.[1]

A larger review of 69 trials of 20 different supplements for hand, hip and knee osteoarthritis put curcumin among the seven supplements with a large effect on pain in the short term. Across the review, the quality of the evidence ranged from very low to high, and the overall analysis was rated very low quality.[2]

A 2025 network meta-analysis of 39 trials with 4,599 people compared seven supplements against placebo. Curcumin showed benefits in some outcomes, but another herbal extract (Boswellia) ranked highest for pain and stiffness — and the authors called for larger, better-designed trials to confirm any of it.[4]

So "turmeric eases knee arthritis pain" gets a C: the direction is consistent, but the trials are small, short and uneven.

Is it as good as anti-inflammatory painkillers?

  • In the 16-trial meta-analysis, five trials compared turmeric with NSAIDs (anti-inflammatory painkillers such as ibuprofen). The effects on pain and function were similar, and turmeric had 12 % fewer adverse events than NSAIDs.[1]
  • A separate meta-analysis of 11 trials (1,258 people) found curcuminoids gave better pain scores than NSAIDs — but only three of its trials used NSAIDs as the comparison.[3]

With so few head-to-head trials, "turmeric works as well as painkillers" also gets a C. Don't stop a medicine your doctor prescribed on the strength of these.

Does a bigger dose work better?

No sign of it. The 11-trial meta-analysis split the trials into high-dose (1,000 mg a day or more) and low-dose (less than 1,000 mg a day) groups and found no difference in pain relief or side effects between them.[3] "A higher dose works better" gets a D.

Does it protect cartilage?

The 16-trial meta-analysis found no differences between turmeric and placebo or NSAIDs in imaging or biochemical outcomes.[1] No trial has shown that turmeric rebuilds cartilage or slows arthritis down, so "turmeric protects cartilage" gets a D.

Does the relief last?

The trials lasted up to 16 weeks.[1] Across all 20 supplements in the 69-trial review, none gave clinically important pain relief in the long term.[2] Lasting benefit simply hasn't been shown — a C.

Is it safe?

  • In the trials, turmeric's side-effect rate was similar to placebo.[1]
  • Outside trials, supplement-related liver injury has risen over three decades and now makes up 20 % of drug-induced liver injury cases in a US registry; turmeric is named among the major implicated agents.[5]
  • Supplements are not checked the way medicines are, so what's in the bottle may not match the label.[5]

What these studies cannot tell you

  • Short trials. Sixteen weeks says nothing about years of use.[1]
  • Different products. Trials used many different extracts and formulations; the best dose and form are still unknown.[1,3]
  • Mostly small trials with uneven quality — exactly the setting where effects tend to shrink as better trials arrive.[1,2]

Verdict

  • "Turmeric (curcumin) eases knee arthritis pain" — C: helped more than placebo over weeks to months, low certainty.[1,2,4]
  • "Turmeric works as well as painkillers" — C: few head-to-head trials.[1,3]
  • "A higher dose works better" — D.[3]
  • "Turmeric protects cartilage or slows arthritis" — D.[1]
  • "Turmeric gives lasting relief" — C: not shown beyond 16 weeks.[1,2]

What this means in practice

  • If you want to try it, think of it as a short-term add-on, not a cure — and don't expect more from a bigger dose.[1,3]
  • Tell your doctor or pharmacist you take it, especially if you have liver problems or take other medicines.[5]
  • Get your knee checked if it is hot and swollen, locks or gives way, follows an injury, or is getting worse despite treatment.
