Do calcium supplements prevent fractures? What the trials found

Calcium pills are taken by millions of older adults to protect their bones. A surgeon reads the large trials and meta-analyses on calcium, vitamin D and broken bones.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 23 October 2026

The verdict

“Calcium supplements on their own prevent fractures in older adults living at home”

D · Unsupported

Evidence at a glance

ClaimGradeRefs

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • In a meta-analysis of 33 randomised trials with 51,145 adults over 50 living at home, calcium, vitamin D or both were not associated with fewer hip, vertebral or total fractures.
  • In a systematic review of 26 trials, calcium supplements reduced total fractures by about 11% overall, but in the four trials at lowest risk of bias (44,505 people) there was no effect at any site.
  • Calcium raises bone density by only about 0.7–1.8%, and the increase does not keep growing after the first year.
  • Combined daily vitamin D and calcium showed a small benefit in one meta-analysis (6% fewer fractures of any kind) — the evidence is mixed.
  • In the Women's Health Initiative trial, calcium plus vitamin D increased the risk of kidney stones.

"Take your calcium" is probably the most familiar piece of bone-health advice there is, and many people over 50 take a calcium tablet every day expecting it to prevent a broken hip or wrist. Fractures are the outcome that matters, and large randomised trials have counted them directly.

The trials in people living at home

A meta-analysis in JAMA pooled 33 randomised trials with 51,145 adults over 50 who lived in the community rather than in care homes.[1]

  • Calcium alone was not associated with a lower risk of hip fracture compared with placebo or no treatment (risk ratio 1.53, 95% CI 0.97 to 2.42).[1]
  • Calcium with vitamin D was not associated with fewer hip fractures either (risk ratio 1.09, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.39).[1]
  • No significant association was found for non-vertebral, vertebral or total fractures, and results were similar regardless of dose, sex, fracture history, dietary calcium intake or baseline vitamin D level.[1]

The authors concluded that the findings do not support routine use of these supplements in community-dwelling older people.[1]

When you look only at the most reliable trials

A systematic review in the BMJ pooled 26 trials of calcium supplements, with or without vitamin D.[2]

Fracture site Trials (people) Relative risk with calcium (95% CI)
Any fracture 20 (58,573) 0.89 (0.81 to 0.96)
Vertebral 12 (48,967) 0.86 (0.74 to 1.00)
Hip 13 (56,648) 0.95 (0.76 to 1.18)
Forearm 8 (51,775) 0.96 (0.85 to 1.09)

At first glance that is an 11% reduction in total fractures. But the authors found signs of bias toward calcium in the published data, and in the four trials at lowest risk of bias (44,505 people) there was no effect on fracture at any site.[2] Only one trial — in frail elderly women in residential care with low calcium intake and low vitamin D — showed clear reductions in fracture.[2] Their summary: evidence that calcium supplements prevent fractures is weak and inconsistent.[2]

What about calcium from food?

The same review found only two eligible randomised trials of dietary calcium (262 people in total), and concluded there is no clinical trial evidence that increasing calcium from food prevents fractures.[2] In cohort studies, most found no link between dietary calcium, milk or dairy intake and fracture risk.[2]

Why it does not do more

Calcium does change bone density — just not by much. A meta-analysis of 59 trials found that calcium from food or supplements raised bone density by 0.7–1.8%, and the increase did not grow after the first year.[5] The authors judged this unlikely to lead to a clinically significant reduction in fractures.[5]

The case for calcium with vitamin D

Not every analysis is negative. A 2019 meta-analysis of 6 trials (49,282 people) of daily vitamin D (400–800 IU) plus calcium (1,000–1,200 mg) found a 6% lower risk of any fracture and a 16% lower risk of hip fracture.[3] Vitamin D alone did not reduce fractures in the same analysis.[3] In the largest single trial, the Women's Health Initiative (36,282 postmenopausal women followed for about 7 years), calcium plus vitamin D slightly raised hip bone density but did not significantly reduce hip fractures in the main analysis — and it increased the risk of kidney stones (hazard ratio 1.17).[4]

So for the combination, the evidence is mixed and any benefit is small, which is why it is graded C.

Verdict

For healthy older adults living at home, calcium supplements on their own have not been shown to prevent fractures: the best-designed trials found no effect. Graded D. The picture is different for some groups that these trials do not represent well, such as frail people in residential care.[2]

What this means in practice

  • A calcium tablet is not a fracture-prevention plan by itself. If you are worried about your bones, ask a doctor about your fracture risk and whether a bone density scan makes sense.
  • If calcium and vitamin D were prescribed together with an osteoporosis treatment, do not stop them on your own — discuss it with the doctor who prescribed them.
  • Drinking more milk or eating more dairy has not been shown to prevent fractures either.[2]
  • If you have had kidney stones, mention it before starting calcium supplements.[4]
