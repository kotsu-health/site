Are falls after 60 just part of ageing? What exercise does to your fall risk

Many people assume falls come with age and nothing can be done. A surgeon reads the Cochrane review of 108 randomised trials with over 23,000 older people — which exercises cut falls, which are uncertain, and what happens if you stop.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 6 November 2026

The verdict

“Falls after 60 are just part of ageing — nothing you do changes your risk”

D · Unsupported

Key points

  • A 2019 Cochrane review pooled 108 randomised trials with 23,407 people aged 60 and over living in the community; on average they were 76 years old.
  • Exercise of any type reduced the rate of falls by 23% and the number of people who fell by 15% (high-certainty evidence).
  • Balance and functional exercises had the clearest effect (24% fewer falls); programmes combining balance with strength exercises probably cut falls by about a third; Tai Chi may help too.
  • For walking-only, dance or strength-only programmes the effect on falls is uncertain — so 'just walk more' is not the tested answer.
  • In care homes, the benefit disappeared after exercise stopped: it works while you keep doing it.

"I'm getting older — falls just happen." It is a common belief, and an understandable one: according to the largest review on the subject, at least one in three people over 65 living at home falls each year.[1] But "common" is not the same as "unavoidable". Whether exercise changes that risk has been tested in more than a hundred randomised trials.

What the biggest review found

A 2019 Cochrane review collected randomised trials that tested exercise on its own to prevent falls in people aged 60 and over living in the community: 108 trials with 23,407 participants in 25 countries.[1] On average they were 76 years old, and 77% were women.[1]

Compared with a control activity not expected to prevent falls, exercise of any type:

  • reduced the rate of falls by 23% (59 trials, 12,981 people; high-certainty evidence);[1]
  • reduced the number of people who fell at least once by 15% (63 trials, 13,518 people; high-certainty evidence).[1]

To put that in everyday terms, the review estimated that for every 1,000 people followed for a year, exercise would mean about 195 fewer falls.[1] The effect was similar whether or not trials had picked people already at high risk of falling.[1]

Not all exercise is the same

The type of exercise mattered.[1]

  • Balance and functional exercises — standing and moving in ways that challenge your balance, such as stepping, turning, getting up from a chair or standing on one leg — reduced the rate of falls by 24% (39 trials, high-certainty evidence).[1]
  • Several types combined, most often balance and functional exercises plus strength (resistance) exercises, probably reduced the rate of falls by 34% (moderate-certainty evidence).[1]
  • Tai Chi reduced the number of people who fell by 20% (high-certainty evidence); its effect on the overall rate of falls is less certain.[1]
  • For programmes that were mainly strength training, dance or walking, the review could not say whether falls were reduced.[1] That does not mean they are useless for health — only that, on their own, they have not been shown to prevent falls.

What about broken bones and safety?

Exercise may reduce the number of people who break a bone in a fall (relative risk 0.73), but this comes from only 10 trials and is low-certainty evidence.[1] Side effects were mostly minor muscle and joint complaints; across the trials that reported them, two serious events were recorded.[1] Safety reporting was often incomplete, so this is reassuring rather than definitive.

It works while you keep doing it

A 2025 Cochrane review of people living in care facilities found that exercise probably reduced falls while the programme was running — but once exercise stopped, there was no lasting effect on the rate of falls (high-certainty evidence).[2] Balance is a capacity you maintain, not a course you complete.

What guidelines say

The 2022 World Falls Guidelines, written by an international group of experts, recommend that all older adults be advised on falls prevention and physical activity, and that people at high risk be offered a full falls risk assessment leading to a personalised plan.[3]

Verdict

  • "Falls after 60 are just part of ageing — nothing you do changes your risk" — D. High-certainty evidence from dozens of trials says exercise lowers the rate of falls.[1]
  • "Exercise reduces falls in older people living at home" — A.[1]
  • "Balance and functional exercise reduces falls; adding strength probably helps more" — A for balance and functional work, with the combined-programme estimate at moderate certainty.[1]
  • "Tai Chi reduces the number of people who fall" — B: high certainty for fallers, less certain for the overall rate of falls.[1]
  • "Walking, dance or strength-only programmes prevent falls on their own" — C: the effect is uncertain.[1]
  • "Exercise reduces fall-related fractures" — C: a possible benefit from few trials.[1]
  • "The benefit lasts after you stop" — D in care-home residents.[2]

What this means in practice

  • If you want to lower your fall risk, choose exercise that challenges your balance — and ideally combine it with strength work.[1] Walking is good for many things, but on its own it is not the tested answer for falls.[1]
  • Keep it going: in the trials, the benefit belonged to people who were still exercising.[2]
  • If you have already fallen, feel unsteady, or are worried about falling, ask your doctor about a falls risk assessment — the guidelines recommend one for people at high risk.[3]
