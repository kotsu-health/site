Does shoulder impingement surgery work? What the placebo-surgery trials show

Arthroscopic subacromial decompression — 'shaving the bone' for shoulder impingement — was one of the most common shoulder operations. A surgeon reads the two trials that compared it with placebo surgery, one followed for ten years, and the reviews and guideline that came after.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 15 November 2026

The verdict

“Shoulder impingement surgery relieves pain better than placebo surgery”

D · Unsupported

Key points

  • Two randomised trials compared subacromial decompression with placebo surgery (an arthroscopy in which the bone and soft tissue were not removed); neither found a meaningful difference.
  • In the UK trial of 313 people, all of whom had already tried exercise therapy and at least one steroid injection, shoulder scores at six months were 32.7 after decompression and 34.2 after placebo surgery.
  • In the Finnish trial of 210 people, there was still no difference in shoulder pain at rest or with arm activity ten years later — against placebo surgery or against exercise therapy.
  • A Cochrane review rated the evidence of no important benefit over placebo as high certainty; a BMJ guideline panel made a strong recommendation against offering the operation for this diagnosis.
  • Many people do improve — including after placebo surgery — so feeling better after an operation does not show that the operation caused it. Which non-surgical treatment works best is still uncertain.

"Shoulder impingement" — pain at the top and side of the shoulder when you lift your arm, reach overhead or lie on that side — is one of the most common reasons adults see a doctor about a shoulder. For years, if exercises and injections didn't help, a common next step was keyhole surgery called arthroscopic subacromial decompression: the surgeon shaves bone spurs and soft tissue from the underside of the acromion (the bony roof of the shoulder) to "make more room" for the tendons.[1]

The idea makes mechanical sense. But it was only properly tested in the 2010s, when two groups of surgeons did something unusual: they compared it with a placebo operation.

The trials with a fake operation

In both trials, some patients had the real decompression and others had an arthroscopy only — the camera went in, the shoulder was looked at, but no bone or soft tissue was removed. Patients were not told which they had.[1,2]

  • The UK trial (CSAW) randomised 313 people at 32 hospitals. Everyone had had shoulder pain for at least three months and had already tried exercise therapy and at least one steroid injection — the people who would normally be offered this operation.[1]
  • At six months, the Oxford Shoulder Score (0 = worst, 48 = best) was 32.7 after decompression and 34.2 after the placebo operation — no difference between them.[1]
  • The Finnish trial (FIMPACT) randomised 210 people aged 35 to 65 with symptoms for more than three months to decompression, placebo surgery or exercise therapy.[2,3]
  • At five years there was no difference that mattered between real and placebo surgery in pain at rest or with arm activity.[2]
  • At ten years, with 87% of participants still followed up, the difference in pain was 1.5 points at rest and 3.2 points with arm activity on a 0–100 scale — far below the 15 points the researchers had set as the smallest difference a patient would notice.[3]

What the reviews concluded

  • A Cochrane review of eight trials (1,062 people) rated the evidence as high certainty: compared with placebo, decompression gives no improvement in pain, shoulder function or quality of life up to one year.[4]
  • In the placebo-controlled trials, average pain at one year was 2.9 out of 10 after placebo surgery and only 0.26 points better after the real operation.[4]
  • A second systematic review reached the same conclusion with high certainty for pain and function, and found no important benefit over exercise therapy either.[5]
  • A BMJ guideline panel of patients, clinicians and methodologists then made a strong recommendation against offering the operation to adults with this kind of shoulder pain.[6]
  • An umbrella review of ten common elective orthopaedic operations placed subacromial decompression among those where trial evidence showed no benefit over non-operative care.[7]

So "subacromial decompression relieves shoulder impingement pain better than placebo surgery" gets a D.

Surgery vs exercise therapy

The Finnish trial also had a group who had no surgery at all, just a supervised exercise programme:

  • At ten years, the difference in pain between decompression and exercise therapy was 4.0 points at rest and 9.4 with arm activity (0–100 scale) — not significant, and below the 15-point threshold.[3]
  • By two years, 88% of the decompression group, 88% of the placebo-surgery group and 90% of the exercise group had gone back to work.[8]
  • The Cochrane review found probably no clinically important benefit of surgery over exercise or other non-operative treatment for treatment success (moderate certainty).[4]

That makes "decompression gives a better long-term result than exercise therapy" a D as well.

Why people feel better after the operation

Many people do feel better after surgery — and that's exactly why the placebo trials were needed:

  • In the Cochrane analysis, treatment was judged a success by 655 per 1,000 people after placebo surgery and 708 per 1,000 after the real operation — a difference the review could not distinguish from chance.[4]
  • In CSAW, both surgical groups did slightly better than people who had no treatment, but the difference was not clinically important, and the authors suggested it might come from a placebo effect or from the physiotherapy given after surgery.[1]

So "many people improve after surgery even when nothing is removed" gets a B: the pattern is consistent, but why it happens isn't settled.

The risks

  • In the trials, adverse events such as frozen shoulder or minor surgical complications occurred in about 3% of people, with too few events to say whether the risk differs between groups.[4]
  • In large surgical registries (which also included other shoulder arthroscopies), serious adverse events within 30 days — such as deep infection, blood clots in the lung or nerve injury — occurred in about 0.5% to 0.6% of operations.[4]
  • The second review estimated about six serious harms per 1,000 patients (moderate certainty).[5]
  • In CSAW, all six study-related complications were frozen shoulders, two in each group.[1]

What these studies cannot tell you

  • The trials excluded full-thickness rotator cuff tears.[1,4] They say nothing about repairing a torn tendon, a dislocated shoulder, a fracture, or shoulder arthritis — those are different problems with different evidence.
  • The participants had pain that had built up over time, not pain after a fall or injury.[1,6]
  • Results are averages. The trials can't rule out that some small, still-unidentified group benefits.
  • Which alternative is best is uncertain. The guideline panel noted substantial uncertainty about the best non-surgical treatment.[6]

Verdict

  • "Subacromial decompression relieves shoulder impingement pain better than placebo surgery" — D.[1,2,3,4,5]
  • "Decompression gives a better long-term result than exercise therapy" — D.[3,4,5,8]
  • "Many people improve after surgery even when nothing is removed" — B.[1,4]
  • "The operation carries a small risk of serious harm (around 1 in 200)" — B.[4,5]
  • "We know which non-surgical treatment works best" — C.[6]

What this means in practice

  • If you are offered decompression for impingement, it is reasonable to ask what the operation would add over the arthroscopy-only and exercise groups in these trials — in the best trials, the answer was nothing measurable.[1,3,4]
  • Exercise therapy did as well as surgery over ten years in the Finnish trial, without the operation's risks.[3]
  • Time and patience matter: in every group, including placebo surgery, many people improved.[3,4]
  • Get your shoulder checked if the pain started with a fall or injury, if you suddenly can't lift your arm, if the shoulder looks deformed, or if you have numbness or weakness down the arm — those need an assessment, because they may be something other than impingement.
