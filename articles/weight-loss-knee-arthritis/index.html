Does losing weight take pressure off arthritic knees? The '4 pounds' claim, checked

You may have heard that every pound you lose takes four pounds off your knees. A surgeon reads the gait study behind that number, two randomised trials of diet and exercise in 1,277 people with knee arthritis, and a meta-analysis, to see what weight loss really does for knee pain.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 10 November 2026

The verdict

“Every pound you lose takes about four pounds of load off your knee with each step”

B · Moderate

Key points

  • In 142 overweight or obese older adults with knee arthritis, each 1 kg of weight lost was associated with about 40 N less compressive force in the knee per step — roughly a four-to-one ratio.
  • In the 18-month IDEA trial (454 overweight or obese adults aged 55 or older with knee arthritis), diet plus exercise led to about 11% weight loss and less knee pain and better function than exercise alone.
  • A meta-analysis of four randomised trials found that physical disability improved when weight was reduced by more than about 5%; the effect on pain was small and less predictable.
  • In a large community trial of 823 people, diet plus exercise led to about 8% weight loss but only a small difference in knee pain, of uncertain clinical importance.
  • These studies were done in people who were overweight or obese; they do not show that people at a healthy weight should lose weight for their knees.

"Lose a bit of weight — it'll take the pressure off your knees." Many people with knee arthritis hear this, often along with a striking number: every pound you lose takes four pounds off your knees. Where does that number come from, and does losing weight actually make an arthritic knee hurt less?

Where the "four pounds" comes from

The figure comes from a gait study inside a US trial of diet and exercise. Researchers measured how 142 sedentary, overweight or obese older adults with knee arthritis walked, using three-dimensional gait analysis, and related the forces in the knee to how much weight each person had lost over 18 months.[1]

  • Each 1 kg of weight lost was associated with about 40 N less compressive force in the knee during walking.[1]
  • Since 1 kg of body weight is about 9.8 N, that is roughly a four-to-one ratio: each unit of weight lost went with about four units less knee load.[1]
  • The authors wrote that each pound lost would mean a fourfold reduction in the load on the knee per step — and that, added up over thousands of steps a day, this would appear to be clinically meaningful.[1]

So the claim is real, and it comes from measured walking. But it is a link between weight lost and estimated knee forces in one group of 142 people, not a direct measurement of pain.[1] That is why it gets a B rather than an A.

Does weight loss actually reduce knee pain?

Lower load is a mechanism. What matters to most people is whether the knee hurts less. That has been tested in randomised trials.

The IDEA trial. 454 overweight or obese adults aged 55 or older, with knee pain and arthritis on X-ray, were randomly assigned to diet plus exercise, diet alone, or exercise alone for 18 months.[2]

  • Average weight loss was 10.6 kg (11.4%) with diet plus exercise, 8.9 kg (9.5%) with diet alone, and 1.8 kg (2.0%) with exercise alone.[2]
  • Knee compressive forces were lower in the diet group than in the exercise group.[2]
  • The diet-plus-exercise group had less pain and better function than both other groups, and better physical quality of life than the exercise group.[2]

A meta-analysis of trials. A review pooled four randomised trials (454 patients) in which weight change was the only difference between groups.[3]

  • With an average weight loss of about 6 kg, there was a small improvement in pain and in physical disability.[3]
  • Disability improved significantly when weight fell by more than about 5%; the effect on pain was present but not predictable.[3]

Does more weight loss help more?

In a follow-up analysis of 240 IDEA participants grouped by how much weight they actually lost (under 5%, 5–10%, 10–20% and over 20%), pain, function, walking distance and knee forces all improved more with greater weight loss.[4] Those who lost 20% or more had 25% less pain than those who lost 10–20%.[4]

This is a useful pattern, but the groups were formed by how much weight people managed to lose, not by random assignment — people who lose more weight may differ in other ways. So it gets a C.

What happens outside a university trial?

A later trial took a similar diet-and-exercise programme into community settings in North Carolina, with 823 people aged 50 or older with knee arthritis and overweight or obesity.[5]

  • After 18 months, weight fell by about 7.7 kg (8%) in the programme group and 1.7 kg (2%) in the control group.[5]
  • Knee pain was lower in the programme group, but the difference was small — 0.6 points on a 0–20 scale, below the 1.6 points the authors took as the minimum clinically important difference.[5]
  • The authors described the difference in pain as statistically significant but of uncertain clinical importance.[5] Most of the study-related adverse events were minor injuries, muscle strains and trips or falls, almost all in the exercise group.[5]

What these studies cannot tell you

  • They studied people who were overweight or obese. None of this shows that someone at a healthy weight should lose weight for their knees.[1,2,3,5]
  • The "four pounds" is an estimate of force during walking, not a measurement of cartilage or pain.[1]
  • Effects on pain were modest on average, and in the community trial they were small.[3,5]
  • Most trials combined diet with exercise, so it is hard to separate the effect of weight loss from the effect of being more active.[2,5]

Verdict

  • "Every pound you lose takes about four pounds of load off your knee with each step" — B: a measured association in a gait study of 142 people, consistent with lower knee forces after diet-induced weight loss in a randomised trial.[1,2]
  • "Diet-induced weight loss plus exercise helps knee pain and function more than exercise alone" — B: one large randomised trial.[2]
  • "Losing more than about 5% of body weight improves disability" — B: a meta-analysis of four trials; the pain effect was small.[3]
  • "The more weight lost, the bigger the benefit" — C: a dose–response pattern, but in groups that were not randomised.[4]
  • "Weight loss can't help a knee that already has arthritis" — D: in trials of people who already had knee arthritis, weight loss with exercise improved function and, to a varying degree, pain.[2,3]

What this means in practice

  • If you have knee arthritis and are carrying extra weight, losing weight is one of the few things shown in trials to help — and in these trials it was combined with exercise.[2,3]
  • Set expectations honestly: on average the benefit on pain was modest, and larger in programmes with more weight loss.[3,4,5]
  • If your knee is hot, swollen, locking or giving way, or pain keeps rising, get it assessed in person rather than relying on weight loss alone.
