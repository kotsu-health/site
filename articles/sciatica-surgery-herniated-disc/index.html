Does a herniated disc with sciatica need surgery?

Leg pain from a slipped disc often comes with the word "surgery". A surgeon reads the randomised trials of early surgery vs waiting, the trial of surgery for sciatica that has lasted months, and the reviews of discs that shrink on their own.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 25 November 2026

The verdict

“Sciatica from a herniated disc needs surgery”

Key points

  • In a randomised trial of 283 people with 6–12 weeks of severe sciatica, early surgery relieved leg pain faster, but disability over the first year did not differ and 95% in both groups reported recovery at 1 year.
  • Of the 142 people assigned to keep waiting with conservative care, 44% eventually had surgery over two years — so most never needed it. Outcomes were still similar at two years.
  • For sciatica that had already lasted 4–12 months, a trial of 128 people found less leg pain at 6 months after microdiscectomy than with 6 more months of non-surgical care (2.8 vs 5.2 out of 10).
  • Herniated discs can shrink on their own: in a systematic review, about 70% of extruded and 96% of sequestered discs regressed with conservative treatment, but only 13% of bulging discs.
  • Sudden weakness, numbness in the groin, or new trouble controlling bladder or bowels are not a wait-and-see situation — they need urgent assessment.

Pain that shoots from the lower back down one leg — sciatica — is often blamed on a "slipped" or herniated disc pressing on a nerve. Once an MRI shows the disc, many people assume the next step is an operation to take the pressure off.

That question has been tested in randomised trials. Short version: for most people, surgery is a way to get better faster, not the only way to get better. And a herniated disc is one of the few things on an MRI that can shrink by itself.

Early surgery or wait? The Dutch trial

  • In a trial in nine Dutch hospitals, 283 people with severe sciatica for 6 to 12 weeks were randomly assigned to early surgery (microdiscectomy, removing the part of the disc pressing on the nerve) or to prolonged conservative care, with surgery later only if needed.[1]
  • In the early-surgery group, 89% had the operation, after a mean of 2.2 weeks.[1]
  • Leg pain improved faster with early surgery, and people reported recovering faster (hazard ratio 1.97).[1]
  • But disability over the first year did not differ significantly between the groups — and after one year, 95% in both groups reported recovery.[1]
  • At two years, outcomes were still similar. The short-term advantage of surgery was no longer significant by six months. In the conservative group, 44% eventually had surgery — which means more than half never did. About 20% of all patients reported an unsatisfactory outcome at two years, whichever group they were in.[2]

So "sciatica from a herniated disc needs surgery" gets a D: in this trial, people who waited ended up in the same place a year later, and most of them without an operation.[1,2] "Surgery relieves leg pain faster" gets a B.[1,2]

What if the pain has already lasted for months?

The Dutch trial was about sciatica in its first few months. A later trial asked about sciatica that hadn't settled.

  • In a single-centre Canadian trial, 128 people with sciatica lasting 4 to 12 months and a disc herniation at the bottom two levels of the spine were randomised to microdiscectomy or to 6 months of standardised non-surgical care, with surgery afterwards if needed.[3]
  • At 6 months, leg pain was 2.8 out of 10 after surgery vs 5.2 with non-surgical care (from about 8 at the start in both groups).[3]
  • Disability and pain at 12 months pointed the same way. 22 of 64 people in the non-surgical group (34%) crossed over to surgery. Nine people had adverse events linked to surgery, and one needed a repeat operation for a recurrent herniation.[3]

So for sciatica that has already lasted more than four months, "surgery helps" gets a B — one well-run but single-centre trial.[3] Timing matters: the question is not "surgery or never", it is "how long has it lasted, and how much is it affecting your life?"

Can a herniated disc really shrink on its own?

  • A systematic review of 31 studies of people treated without surgery who had at least two scans found the disc material regressed in about 96% of sequestered discs (a fragment that has broken off), 70% of extruded discs, 41% of protrusions and 13% of bulging discs. It completely resolved in 43% of sequestered and 15% of extruded discs.[4]
  • A 2023 meta-analysis that added newer studies (16 studies, 360 cases) found much the same: about 93% for sequestered, 70% for extruded, 53% for protruded and 13% for bulging discs, at a mean of 11.5 months to the follow-up scan. Symptom improvement went along with regression.[5]

The surprise: the bigger, more dramatic-looking herniations were the ones most likely to shrink.[4,5] So "a herniated disc can shrink on its own" gets a B — consistent across reviews, though these are observational imaging studies, not trials.[4,5] (For why a scan finding doesn't always explain back pain, see Does a disc bulge on MRI explain your back pain?.)

What these studies cannot tell you

  • They don't apply to emergencies. The trials enrolled people with sciatica, not people with rapidly worsening weakness or loss of bladder or bowel control, which need urgent assessment.
  • Conservative care was not "do nothing". Both trials used structured non-surgical care, with surgery available if needed.[1,3]
  • The trials were of one kind of surgery (microdiscectomy) for disc herniation at the lower lumbar levels; they don't cover spinal stenosis or other causes of leg pain.[1,3]

Verdict

  • "Sciatica from a herniated disc needs surgery" — D: 1-year and 2-year outcomes were similar with waiting, and most who waited never had surgery.[1,2]
  • "Surgery relieves sciatica leg pain faster" — B.[1,2]
  • "Surgery helps sciatica that has lasted more than 4 months" — B (one single-centre trial).[3]
  • "A herniated disc can shrink on its own" — B, most often for the larger herniations.[4,5]

What this means in practice

  • An MRI showing a herniated disc is not, by itself, a reason for surgery. In the trials, most people with early sciatica improved without an operation.[1,2]
  • Surgery is a reasonable choice for faster relief, especially when leg pain is severe or has gone on for months — it's a decision about speed and how much the pain is costing you, made with your doctor.[1,3]
  • Get urgent help for new weakness in the leg or foot, numbness in the groin or saddle area, or new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels.
