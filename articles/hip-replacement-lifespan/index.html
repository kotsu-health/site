How long does a hip replacement really last?

Many people hear that a new hip wears out after 10 or 15 years. A surgeon reads the Lancet meta-analysis of more than 215,000 hip replacements in national registries and a UK study of 63,000 patients, including the less comfortable 25-year figure.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 7 November 2026

The verdict

“A hip replacement only lasts 10–15 years”

D · Unsupported

Evidence at a glance

ClaimGradeRefs

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • In national registry data (215,676 total hip replacements), 89.4% were still in place, without revision, 15 years after surgery.
  • A UK study of 63,158 people found 10-year implant survival of 95.6% and 20-year survival of 85.0% after total hip replacement.
  • Over longer periods the picture is less rosy: registry data suggest about 70% of hips last 20 years and about 58% last 25 years — but those long-term figures come from a single registry and older implants.
  • Age at surgery matters: the lifetime risk of a revision after hip or knee replacement was about 5% for people over 70, rising to as much as 35% for men in their early 50s.
  • 'Survival' means the implant was not replaced; it does not measure pain or function, and it cannot predict any one person's result.

"A new hip only lasts 10 or 15 years, so put it off as long as you can." Patients ask surgeons how long a hip replacement will last many times a day — the authors of the largest review on the subject say exactly that.[1] The answer has been measured, in very large numbers, and it is better than the saying — but not as simple as "forever".

What the registries show

Several countries record every joint replacement in a national registry and follow each implant until it is revised (taken out or replaced). A systematic review in The Lancet pooled registry reports on 215,676 total hip replacements done for osteoarthritis.[1] Without revision, 89.4% were still in place at 15 years.[1]

A UK population study followed 63,158 people who had a total hip replacement between 1991 and 2011, for up to 20 years.[2] Implant survival was 95.6% at 10 years and 85.0% at 20 years.[2]

So a hip replacement lasting only 10–15 years is not what these large datasets show for most people.

The longer view is less comfortable

The same review also looked further out — and here the numbers drop.[1]

  • In registry data, about 70% of hip replacements were still in place at 20 years and about 58% at 25 years.[1]
  • Published case series from individual hospitals looked better — about 78% at 25 years — but the authors judged the registry figures less likely to be biased.[1]
  • The 20- and 25-year registry figures came from one country's registry (Finland) only, and from hips put in before 2003, many with components no longer in common use.[1]

Hip implant combinations that were still in common use at the time showed 94.9% survival at 15 years in the same registry reports, and the authors expected revision rates to fall with better implants.[1] That is a reasonable expectation, not yet a measured 25-year result — which is why the "just over half last 25 years" figure is graded C: it is the best estimate available, but it rests on one registry and older implants.

Why age at surgery matters

The UK study turned survival into a lifetime risk of revision, taking into account how long people of each age are likely to live.[2]

  • For people over 70 at the time of hip or knee replacement, the lifetime risk of a revision was about 5%, with no difference between men and women.[2]
  • Below 70, the risk rose the younger people were: up to 35% for men in their early 50s, and about 15 percentage points lower for women of the same age.[2]
  • In people who had surgery before 60, the median time to revision, among those revised, was 4.4 years.[2]

A younger, more active person has more years in which something can go wrong. That is the part of the "it won't last" worry the evidence does support.

What these numbers cannot tell you

  • "Survival" is not the same as a good hip. It counts only whether the implant was replaced. The review cites work showing that 7–23% of people who were never revised could still be counted as failures if long-term moderate-to-severe pain were the measure.[1]
  • They describe older implants and older practice. Hips followed for 20–25 years were put in decades ago.[1]
  • They are averages. The pooled figures were not adjusted for age, sex or implant type, and the authors say there is not yet enough information to calculate exactly how long a hip replacement will last.[1] These are observational data, so they show what happened, not what will happen for one person.

Verdict

  • "A hip replacement only lasts 10–15 years" — D. About 89% were still in place at 15 years in registry data, and 85% at 20 years in a UK population study.[1,2]
  • "Most total hip replacements last 15–20 years" — B: consistent, very large observational datasets, but on older implants.[1,2]
  • "Only just over half last 25 years" — C: the best available estimate, from a single registry and implants mostly no longer used.[1]
  • "Younger patients are more likely to need a revision" — B: lifetime revision risk about 5% over 70, up to 35% for men in their early 50s.[2]

What this means in practice

  • The fear that a new hip will need replacing within 10–15 years should not, on its own, decide the timing of surgery when arthritis is severe; most implants last longer.[1,2]
  • Over a very long horizon — 25 years or more — a second operation becomes a real possibility, and the younger you are, the higher your lifetime chance of one.[1,2] That trade-off is worth talking through with your surgeon.
  • These studies looked at hip replacements for osteoarthritis.[1] Whether and when surgery makes sense depends on your symptoms, your X-rays and what other treatment has achieved — a decision for an in-person assessment, not a number.
