Does "Japanese walking" (3 minutes fast, 3 minutes slow) actually work?

"Japanese walking" — alternating 3 minutes of fast and 3 minutes of slow walking — is a viral fitness trend. A surgeon reads the trials: what it did for fitness and leg strength compared with ordinary walking, and what has never been tested.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 20 November 2026

The verdict

“Japanese walking (3 minutes fast, 3 minutes slow) gets you fitter than ordinary walking”

B · Moderate

Key points

  • "Japanese walking" is interval walking training: at least 5 rounds of 3 minutes fast and 3 minutes slow, at least 4 days a week.
  • In a randomized trial of 246 adults (average age 63), 5 months of interval walking raised thigh strength by 13–17% and fitness by 8–9% — more than walking 8,000 steps a day at a moderate pace.
  • A Danish trial in 32 people with type 2 diabetes found the same direction: fitness rose about 16% with interval walking and did not change with energy-matched continuous walking.
  • A 2025 trial in 50 people with diabetes and weak legs found no extra gain in knee strength over continuous walking, although walking speed improved more — so the leg-strength claim is mixed.
  • No trial has tested whether it prevents disease, fractures or early death; most studies come from one research group.

"Japanese walking" is all over social media: walk fast for 3 minutes, slow for 3 minutes, repeat. It is presented as a smarter replacement for 10,000 steps. Unlike most viral routines, this one comes from real research — a programme called interval walking training, studied for about two decades.

The original protocol: at least 5 rounds of 3 minutes slow walking (around 40% of your peak walking capacity) followed by 3 minutes fast walking (above 70%), on at least 4 days a week.[1] Short version: it beat ordinary walking for fitness in two randomized trials, the leg-strength result is mixed, and nothing about long-term disease or lifespan has been tested.

Fitness: the best-supported claim

  • The first trial. 246 adults (60 men, 186 women, average age 63) were randomly assigned to no training, moderate continuous walking (at least 8,000 steps a day, 4+ days a week), or interval walking, for 5 months.[1]
  • In the interval group, peak aerobic capacity rose by 8% (cycling test) and 9% (walking test) — significantly more than in the continuous-walking group. Resting systolic blood pressure also fell more.[1]
  • A second country, a second trial. In Denmark, 32 people with type 2 diabetes were randomized to no training, continuous walking or interval walking for 4 months, with energy expenditure matched between the walking groups. Fitness (VO₂max) rose 16% with interval walking and did not change with continuous walking; body weight and fat fell only in the interval group.[2]
  • What drives it. In 679 middle-aged and older people (average age 65) who did 5 months of interval walking, fitness rose 14% on average. The gain tracked the fast minutes per week — it climbed up to about 50 minutes of fast walking a week and then levelled off. Slow or total walking time didn't predict the benefit.[3] This study had no comparison group, so it shows a dose pattern, not proof.

Two randomized trials, from two countries, point the same way. Both were small to moderate, so this is a B, not an A.

Leg strength: the studies disagree

  • In the 246-person trial, interval walking raised isometric knee extension strength by 13% and knee flexion by 17%, more than continuous walking.[1]
  • But a 2025 randomized trial in 50 people with diabetes and weak thigh muscles (both groups aiming for 120 minutes a week, for 5 months) found no difference in knee-extension strength between interval and continuous walking. Interval walking did improve walking speed and physical quality of life more.[4]

One positive trial, one negative trial in a different group: C. If your main goal is stronger legs, walking of any kind is not a substitute for strength training.

Bones: an early signal

In 234 postmenopausal women (average age 64) not taking osteoporosis medicine, 5 months of interval walking raised bone density by 1.8% at the spine and 1.0% at the hip neck — but only in the women who started with lower bone density; there was no change in the rest. There was no control group.[5] That makes it a C: possible, not shown.

What hasn't been tested

A 2024 review by researchers who developed and tested the method says that keeping people walking long-term is still a challenge and that long-term, real-world studies are needed to see its effect on hard endpoints (such as disease events).[6] So "Japanese walking helps you live longer" is a D — not disproven, just not studied.

Also worth knowing: most of the trials come from one research group and its collaborators.[1,3,5,6] That's normal for a young method, but independent replication is limited.

Verdict

  • "Japanese walking gets you fitter than ordinary walking" — B: two randomized trials agree.[1,2]
  • "It builds leg strength better than ordinary walking" — C: one trial yes, one no.[1,4]
  • "It strengthens your bones" — C: uncontrolled, and only in women with lower bone density.[5]
  • "It helps you live longer or prevents disease" — D: not tested.[6]

What this means in practice

  • If you already walk, swapping some steady minutes for 3-minute fast stretches is a reasonable, research-backed way to get more fitness from the same time. The benefit followed the fast minutes, up to about 50 a week.[3]
  • Do this too: if stronger legs are the goal, add strength exercises; the walking trials disagree on strength.[1,4]
  • If you have heart disease, uncontrolled blood pressure, or painful knees or hips, check with your doctor or physio before adding fast intervals.
