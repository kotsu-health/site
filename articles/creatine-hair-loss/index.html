Does creatine cause hair loss? The one study behind the fear — and the trial that tested it

The creatine-baldness worry traces back to a single 2009 study of rugby players. A surgeon reads it, and the 2025 randomised trial that finally measured hair.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 27 October 2026

The verdict

“Creatine causes hair loss”

D · Unsupported

Evidence at a glance

ClaimGradeRefs
Creatine causes hair lossD · Unsupported2, 3
Creatine raises DHT, the hormone linked to male-pattern hair lossC · Early1, 2, 3
Creatine changes testosterone levelsD · Unsupported2, 3

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • The worry comes from one 2009 crossover trial of 20 college rugby players, in which blood DHT rose by 56% after a week of high-dose creatine loading.
  • That study measured hormones only. It did not look at hair, and its result has not been replicated.
  • In a 2025 randomised trial, 12 weeks of 5 g a day of creatine did not change DHT, testosterone or any measure of hair density or thickness compared with placebo.
  • Most other studies of creatine and testosterone found no change in testosterone.
  • The evidence is still small: no long-term trials and none in men who are already losing their hair.

Creatine is one of the most studied supplements for strength training. It also comes with a stubborn rumour: that it makes you go bald. The rumour is not invented out of nothing — but it rests on a surprisingly thin piece of evidence.

Where the idea came from

In 2009, researchers in South Africa gave 20 college rugby players either creatine or a placebo in a crossover design.[1] The creatine phase started with a high "loading" dose of 25 g a day for 7 days, followed by 5 g a day for 14 days.[1] Testosterone did not change, but DHT — a more potent hormone made from testosterone — rose by 56% after the loading week and stayed 40% above baseline after the maintenance phase.[1]

DHT matters for hair because, in men with a susceptible scalp, it binds to hair follicles and makes them shrink.[3] That link is how "creatine raises DHT" turned into "creatine causes baldness".

Why that study does not settle it

The 2009 trial measured blood hormones only — it never looked at anyone's hair.[1] A later expert review pointed out that 16 men completed it, that DHT stayed within normal clinical limits, and that the creatine group started with lower DHT than the placebo group, which exaggerates the difference.[3] The result has not been replicated, and no study has reported hair loss in humans taking creatine.[3]

The same review counted 12 other studies of creatine and testosterone: 10 found no change, and 2 found small, short-lived increases.[3]

The trial that finally measured hair

In 2025, a 12-week randomised, placebo-controlled trial put the question directly.[2] Forty-five resistance-trained men aged 18 to 40 took either 5 g of creatine monohydrate a day or a placebo; 38 finished.[2] The researchers measured testosterone, free testosterone and DHT, and assessed the hair itself — hair density, follicle counts and hair thickness — with standard scalp-imaging tests.[2]

There was no difference between creatine and placebo in DHT, the DHT-to-testosterone ratio, or any hair measure.[2]

The limits

This is still a small evidence base: one 12-week trial in young men, partly written by researchers who also wrote the expert review.[2,3] There are no long-term trials, and none that focused on men who already have male-pattern hair loss. Those are real gaps — but they are gaps, not evidence of harm.

Verdict

The claim that creatine causes hair loss is graded D — not supported. It grew from a single small study that measured a hormone, not hair, and the only trial that measured hair found no effect.[1,2,3]

What this means in practice

  • If you take creatine for training, the current evidence does not suggest it will make you lose your hair.[2]
  • The trial that measured hair used 5 g a day; the DHT rise in 2009 was seen after a 25 g a day loading phase.[1,2]
  • Male-pattern hair loss is common with or without supplements. If you notice thinning, a doctor or dermatologist can assess it — don't assume the supplement is the cause, or the cure.
  • If you have kidney disease or take medicines that affect the kidneys, talk to your doctor before starting any supplement.
