Are deep squats bad for your knees?

"Don't squat below parallel, it wrecks your knees" is some of the oldest advice in the gym. A surgeon reads the reviews on squat depth, exercise and cartilage, and the studies of people who squat for hours a day at work, to see where the risk really is.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 23 November 2026

The verdict

“Deep squats in training damage your knees”

Key points

  • A review of more than 164 articles on squat depth concluded that concerns about deep squats causing knee cartilage damage or arthritis are unfounded, provided technique is learned and loads are progressed — but this rests mainly on biomechanics and cadaver data, not long-term studies of people who squat in the gym.
  • A systematic review of 9 randomised trials found that knee-loading exercise does not seem to harm cartilage on MRI in people with, or at risk of, knee arthritis; the quality of the evidence was low.
  • Squatting bends the knee further and loads the kneecap joint more than walking does — a load the joint also meets on the stairs and when running.
  • At work, the picture changes: a meta-analysis of 71 studies found physically demanding jobs raised the odds of knee arthritis by about half (odds ratio 1.52), with squatting and kneeling among the activities linked to it.
  • A dose-response meta-analysis found the odds of knee arthritis rose by about 26% for every 5,000 hours of cumulative kneeling or squatting at work (moderate-quality evidence).

"Never go below parallel — deep squats wreck your knees." Generations of gym-goers have heard it, and plenty of people with sore knees avoid squatting altogether. Others, coached online to "ass to grass", squat as deep as they can.

There isn't a long-term trial that randomised people to deep or shallow squats and X-rayed their knees decades later. But there is enough research to see where the evidence points — and where the real risk to knees from squatting has been found. Short version: the squats you do in a workout have not been shown to harm knees; squatting for hours a day, for years, at work has been linked to knee arthritis.

What happens in the knee when you squat deep

  • Squatting bends the knee much further than walking, and activities with more knee bend put more force through the kneecap joint than walking does. A meta-analysis comparing everyday activities found peak forces behind the kneecap of about 0.9 times body weight when walking, 3.2 climbing stairs and 5.2 running in healthy people; the squat studies varied too much to pool into one number.[3]
  • A review of more than 164 articles on squat depth looked at the main worry — that the deepest part of the squat crushes the knee. Based on biomechanical calculations and cadaver knees, the highest compressive forces and stresses behind the kneecap occur at around 90°, not at the bottom. As the knee bends further, the contact area behind the kneecap grows and the back of the thigh rests on the calf, which spreads the load.[1]
  • The authors also pointed out that there are no realistic estimates of knee-joint forces beyond about 50° of knee bend in the deep squat — so the safety argument is built on models and cadavers, not on measuring living knees at the bottom of a squat.[1]

More force is not the same as damage. Cartilage, menisci, ligaments and bone adapt to load, as the same review notes.[1] (For what running does to knees, see Does running ruin your knees?.)

Do deep squats cause cartilage damage or arthritis?

  • The squat-depth review concluded that concerns about deep squats raising the risk of cartilage damage, arthritis or osteochondritis are unfounded, provided technique is learned under supervision and loads are increased gradually.[1]
  • It also argued that half and quarter squats with much heavier loads — which people use because the shorter movement lets them lift more — may load the knees and spine more, not less.[1]
  • A systematic review of 9 randomised trials looked at cartilage on MRI after knee-loading exercise in people with, or at higher risk of, knee arthritis. Most comparisons showed no effect on cartilage thickness, volume or defects, and the authors concluded the exercise does not seem to be harmful to cartilage. The quality of the evidence was low.[2]

So "deep squats in training damage your knees" gets a C: no study has shown harm, but no study has followed gym squatters for decades either — the reassurance comes from biomechanics and short-term trials. "Half squats are safer than deep squats" is also a C: it's a common belief, and the review argued the opposite, but on biomechanical grounds only.[1] And "knee-loading exercise harms cartilage in people with knee arthritis" gets a D — the trials didn't find it, even though their quality was low.[2] (On strength training and arthritic knees more broadly, see Do weights wreck older knees?.)

Where squatting has been linked to knee arthritis: hours a day, at work

The strongest signal doesn't come from the gym at all.

  • A meta-analysis of 80 observational studies found that physically demanding jobs and activities raised the odds of knee arthritis compared with sedentary work — an odds ratio of 1.52 across 71 studies. Lifting, kneeling, climbing, squatting and standing at work were all linked to higher odds; farmers, builders, metal workers and floor layers were among the jobs at risk.[4]
  • A dose-response meta-analysis of 15 studies found the odds of knee arthritis rose by about 26% for every 5,000 hours of cumulative kneeling or squatting at work (odds ratio 1.26), rated moderate-quality evidence.[5]
  • A systematic review of 69 studies from 23 countries found strong and moderate evidence that kneeling, squatting and bending at work raise the risk of arthritis in both men and women.[6]
  • In Beijing, where squatting was a common daily posture, about 40% of men and 68% of women aged 60 or over recalled squatting for at least an hour a day at age 25. The more time they had spent squatting, the more common knee arthritis was; the trend was borderline in statistical terms (P = 0.074 in men, 0.077 in women), but the authors concluded prolonged squatting was a strong risk factor.[7]
  • The same kind of work is linked to meniscus tears: a meta-analysis found squatting at work roughly doubled the risk of meniscal lesions (effect size 2.01), with kneeling similar (2.15); the overall quality of the evidence was moderate to low.[8]

These are observational studies, so they show association, not proof of cause — "squatting or kneeling for hours a day at work is linked to knee arthritis" gets a B. But they point to the same conclusion: what matters is dose. Thousands of hours of squatting and kneeling on the job is a very different exposure from a few sets of squats a few times a week.

What these studies cannot tell you

  • There are no long-term trials of deep vs shallow squats in the gym, with knee arthritis as the outcome. The gym evidence is biomechanics and short-term MRI studies.[1,2]
  • The work studies rely on recall of how much people squatted or knelt, often decades earlier, and many were at high risk of bias.[5,7]
  • They don't apply to an injured or painful knee. A knee that already hurts when you squat needs its own assessment.

Verdict

  • "Deep squats in training damage your knees" — C: no evidence of harm, but no long-term studies of gym squatters.[1,2]
  • "Half or quarter squats are safer for your knees than deep squats" — C: biomechanics only, and it argues the other way.[1]
  • "Knee-loading exercise harms cartilage in people with knee arthritis" — D (low-quality evidence).[2]
  • "Squatting or kneeling for hours a day at work is linked to knee arthritis" — B.[4,5,6,7]
  • "Squatting or kneeling at work is linked to meniscus tears" — B.[8]

What this means in practice

  • In the gym, depth is not the enemy. The squat-depth review's conditions were good technique, learned under supervision, and loads increased step by step.[1] Squat as deep as you can with control and without pain.
  • Don't swap deep squats for very heavy half squats to protect your knees; the review argued that may load the knees more.[1]
  • If your job keeps you squatting or kneeling for hours, that is the exposure linked to knee arthritis and meniscus tears.[4,5,8] Breaks, changing position and kneeling aids are worth discussing with your workplace; the studies did not test which of these helps.
  • Get your knee checked if squatting causes pain that lasts, if the knee swells, locks, catches or gives way, or if it started after a twist or injury — those suggest a problem inside the joint rather than a training question.
