Is weight training bad for older joints? What the trials show

Many people over 50 avoid lifting weights to protect their knees. A surgeon reads three large bodies of evidence on strength training, joint pain and safety.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 21 October 2026

The verdict

“Strength training is bad for older adults' joints”

D · Unsupported

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • A Cochrane review of 121 trials with 6,700 older adults found strength training had a large effect on muscle strength, and people with osteoarthritis reported less pain.
  • In an 18-month trial of 377 adults aged 50 and over with knee arthritis, high-intensity strength training did not increase knee pain or knee joint forces compared with a control group.
  • In a Cochrane review of 44 trials, exercise reduced knee arthritis pain by about 12 points on a 0–100 scale, and no study reported a serious adverse event.
  • Muscle soreness and joint aches are common, and harder programmes caused more minor complaints; heavier was not better for pain.

"At my age, lifting weights will wreck my knees." It is one of the most common reasons people over 50 give for avoiding the gym. Strength training does load the joints — so it is a fair question. Randomised trials have tested it directly.

What happens when older adults lift

A Cochrane review pooled 121 randomised trials with 6,700 older participants; in most, training was done two to three times a week at high intensity.[1] Strength training had a large positive effect on muscle strength, a moderate-to-large effect on getting up from a chair, and a modest improvement in walking speed.[1]

For joints specifically: participants with osteoarthritis reported less pain after training (6 trials, 503 participants).[1] Serious adverse events were rare, and none was reported to be directly caused by the exercise.[1] The authors were candid that adverse events were poorly recorded in many trials, and that joint pain and muscle soreness were reported in many of the studies that monitored them.[1]

The trial that pushed the intensity

The START trial asked the harder question: what if people with painful knee arthritis train heavy? It randomised 377 adults aged 50 and over (mean age 65) to high-intensity strength training, low-intensity strength training, or an attention control, and followed them for 18 months.[2]

Group Knee pain at 18 months (WOMAC, 0–20, lower is better)
High-intensity strength training 5.1
Low-intensity strength training 4.4
Control 4.9

None of these differences was statistically significant, and the force on the knee joint during walking was also no different between groups.[2] So heavy training did not make knees hurt more — but it did not help more than lighter training either.[2] There were more minor (nonserious) adverse events with high intensity: 53, versus 30 with low intensity and 4 in the control group.[2]

Exercise and knee arthritis in general

A Cochrane review of land-based exercise for knee osteoarthritis included 54 trials.[3] High-quality evidence from 44 trials (3,537 participants) showed exercise reduced pain by about 12 points on a 0–100 scale straight after treatment, and function improved by about 10 points.[3] Eight studies reported adverse events — all increased knee or low back pain — and no study reported a serious adverse event.[3]

Verdict

The idea that strength training damages older joints is not supported: in trials, it built strength and was linked to less arthritis pain, without signs of joint harm. What the evidence does show is that soreness is common at the start, and that heavier is not automatically better for pain.[1,2]

What this means in practice

  • In the START trial, heavy training was no better for knee pain than light training, so you do not need to lift heavy to start.[2]
  • Expect some muscle soreness early on; it was common in the trials.[1,2]
  • Pain that is sharp, causes swelling, or keeps getting worse over weeks is different — get the joint assessed before pushing on.
