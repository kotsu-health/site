Do wedge insoles help knee arthritis? What the placebo-controlled trials found

Lateral wedge insoles are meant to take load off the inner knee. When trials compared them with plain flat insoles, the pain benefit disappeared. A surgeon reads the evidence on insoles and knee braces.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 28 October 2026

The verdict

“Wedge insoles relieve knee arthritis pain”

D · Unsupported

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • Lateral wedge insoles tilt the foot to shift load away from the inner (medial) side of the knee, where arthritis is most common.
  • In a 12-month trial of 200 people with medial knee arthritis, 5-degree wedge insoles did no better than flat insoles for pain or cartilage loss.
  • A JAMA meta-analysis of 12 trials found that the apparent benefit came from trials with no-insole controls; against a neutral insole, the difference was about 0.12 points on a 20-point pain scale.
  • A Cochrane review found moderate-quality evidence of no pain benefit for wedged over neutral insoles, and inconclusive evidence for knee braces.
  • Some newer reviews that pool many study designs report benefits, so the question is not completely closed — but the best-controlled trials are negative.

Knee arthritis most often starts on the inner side of the knee. Lateral wedge insoles — slightly thicker at the outer edge of the foot — were designed to tilt the leg and move some of that load outward. The idea makes mechanical sense. The question is whether people actually hurt less.

The trial that compared them with a flat insole

The cleanest test is to give one group wedged insoles and another group identical-looking flat insoles, so that neither knows which they have. A trial in Melbourne did exactly that: 200 people aged 50 or over with mild to moderately severe medial knee arthritis wore full-length 5-degree wedged insoles or flat insoles every day for 12 months.[1]

There was no meaningful difference in knee pain (between-group difference −0.3 points on an 11-point scale), and no difference in cartilage volume on MRI.[1] None of the secondary measures — function, stiffness, quality of life — differed either.[1]

Why some studies looked positive

A meta-analysis in JAMA pooled 12 trials with 885 participants.[2] At first glance, wedges were linked to less pain. But the effect came mainly from trials whose control group got no insole at all, and from trials with a higher risk of bias.[2]

When the analysis was limited to the 7 trials that used a neutral insole as the control, the difference was about 0.12 points on the 20-point WOMAC pain scale — far too small to notice.[2] The authors concluded that the findings do not support wedge insoles for this purpose.[2] A later meta-analysis of wedged versus flat insoles reached the same conclusion for pain and function.[4]

In other words, much of the benefit people feel seems to come from wearing something in the shoe, or from expectation, rather than from the wedge.

What about knee braces?

The Cochrane review of braces and orthoses found the evidence for bracing inconclusive.[3] In the one brace-versus-no-treatment trial with usable 12-month data (117 people), there was no difference in pain, and 24 of 60 people in the brace group stopped using it because it did not help.[3] Other brace studies reported some improvement, but the review rated the evidence as low quality.[3]

A 2022 meta-analysis of 32 controlled trials found that both braces and insoles were associated with improvements in pain and function, with no clear difference between them — but most of those studies were of moderate quality, and many lacked a placebo-type control.[5]

The limits

Not every review agrees. A 2026 review that pooled 92 studies of many designs reported improvements in pain and function with moderately angled (5–10 degree) wedges.[6] Reviews like this mix in studies without a neutral-insole control, which is exactly where the JAMA analysis found the inflated effects.[2,6] The trials designed to rule out placebo effects remain negative.[1,2,3]

Verdict

The claim that wedge insoles relieve knee arthritis pain is graded D: in placebo-controlled trials they did no better than a flat insole.[1,2,3,4] Knee braces are graded C — the evidence is limited and inconclusive.[3,5]

What this means in practice

  • If you already wear wedge insoles and like them, comfort is your call — but in the 12-month trial they did no better than flat insoles for pain or cartilage.[1]
  • A brace may feel supportive for some people, but the evidence that it reduces pain is weak.[3]
  • Insoles and braces are not a substitute for a proper assessment. If knee pain limits your walking, see an orthopaedic surgeon or physiotherapist about the options with better evidence for your situation.
