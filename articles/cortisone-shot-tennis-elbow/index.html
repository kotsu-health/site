Does a cortisone shot help tennis elbow? What the trials show

A steroid injection is one of the most common treatments for tennis elbow. A surgeon reads the randomised trials that followed people for a year — including one with a placebo injection — to see what the shot does in the first weeks, and what it does after that.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 14 November 2026

The verdict

“A cortisone shot fixes tennis elbow”

D · Unsupported

Key points

  • In randomised trials, a cortisone shot relieved tennis elbow pain better than anything else at about six weeks — 92% success in one trial of 185 people.
  • After that the benefit reversed: at one year, 69% of the injection group had a successful outcome, compared with 91% after physiotherapy and 83% with a wait-and-see approach.
  • In a placebo-controlled trial of 165 people, 83% of those given cortisone were completely recovered or much improved at one year, compared with 96% given a placebo injection; recurrence was 54% vs 12%.
  • A systematic review of 41 trials found the same pattern across studies: a large short-term effect, with no injection favoured at 26 and 52 weeks.
  • Most people with tennis elbow improve within a year whatever they are given; the trials did not show that physiotherapy or any other non-surgical treatment beats waiting in the long run.

Tennis elbow — pain on the outside of the elbow when you grip, lift a kettle or turn a door handle — is a common reason to see a doctor, and you don't need to play tennis to get it. One of the most familiar treatments is a cortisone (steroid) injection into the sore spot. It often works within days. The question is what happens next.

Several good randomised trials have followed people for a full year. Their answer is consistent, and it surprises most people.

The first six weeks: the shot wins

  • In a Dutch trial, family doctors' patients with tennis elbow for at least six weeks were randomly given injections, physiotherapy or a wait-and-see approach — 185 people in all. At six weeks, the injection beat both other options on every measure: success rates were 92% with injections, 47% with physiotherapy and 32% with waiting.[2]
  • An Australian trial of 198 people found the same: the injection was significantly better at six weeks.[3]
  • A systematic review in The Lancet pooled the trials: for tennis elbow, a cortisone shot had a large short-term effect on pain compared with no treatment.[1]

So "a cortisone shot relieves tennis elbow pain in the first weeks" gets an A — strong, consistent evidence.

After six weeks: the result flips

  • In the Dutch trial, success at one year was 69% with injections, compared with 91% with physiotherapy and 83% with wait-and-see. The injection group also had a high recurrence rate.[2]
  • In the Australian trial, 47 of 65 people who had done well after the injection later regressed, and long-term outcomes were significantly poorer than after physiotherapy.[3]
  • Across trials, the Lancet review found the effect reversed: at about 26 and 52 weeks, no treatment did better than the injection.[1]

The placebo-controlled trial

The strongest test came from a later Australian trial of 165 people with tennis elbow for more than six weeks. Some got cortisone, others got a placebo injection, and neither the patients nor the assessors knew which. Half of each group also had physiotherapy.[4]

  • At one year, 83% of people given cortisone were completely recovered or much improved, compared with 96% of those given the placebo.[4]
  • The pain came back (after an early improvement) in 54% of the cortisone group, compared with 12% of the placebo group.[4]
  • At 26 weeks, the figures were 55% recovered or much improved with cortisone vs 85% with placebo.[4]

That is why "a cortisone shot fixes tennis elbow for good" gets a D, and "after a cortisone shot, the pain is more likely to come back" gets a B — two trials agree, but they are only two.

Most elbows get better anyway

The less-quoted finding is how many people recover with little or no treatment:

  • 83% of the wait-and-see group in the Dutch trial had a successful outcome at one year.[2]
  • In the Australian trial, most people in both the physiotherapy and the wait-and-see groups reported success at one year.[3]
  • In the placebo trial, 96% of people given only a placebo injection were completely recovered or much improved at a year.[4]

Does physiotherapy beat waiting?

In the short term, sometimes. In the long term, the trials don't show it:

  • Physiotherapy (elbow manipulation and exercise) was better than wait-and-see at six weeks, but there was no difference at 52 weeks; people who had physiotherapy did use fewer other treatments such as anti-inflammatory tablets.[3]
  • In the Dutch trial, physiotherapy did slightly better than waiting, but the difference was not significant.[2]
  • In the placebo trial, adding physiotherapy made no difference at one year (91% vs 88% recovered or much improved).[4]
  • A 2026 network meta-analysis of 27 trials found that some non-surgical treatments relieved pain in the short or medium term, but no treatment showed a significant advantage over placebo beyond 12 weeks.[5]

So "physiotherapy or another non-surgical treatment beats waiting at one year" gets a C — the evidence doesn't support it yet.

What these studies cannot tell you

  • The trials were in adults with pain for at least six weeks, mostly recruited in primary care in Australia and the Netherlands.[2,3,4] They don't tell you about elbow pain after an injury, or pain that is something other than tennis elbow.
  • Why the shot does worse later is not known. The trials show what happened, not the mechanism.
  • Results are averages. Some people who get a shot never have the pain back; the trials can't tell you in advance who they will be.

Verdict

  • "A cortisone shot relieves tennis elbow pain in the first weeks" — A.[1,2,3,4]
  • "A cortisone shot fixes tennis elbow for good" — D: at six months and a year, people who had the shot did worse than those who had physiotherapy, waited, or got a placebo.[1,2,3,4]
  • "After a cortisone shot, the pain is more likely to come back" — B.[3,4]
  • "Most tennis elbow improves within a year without an injection" — B.[2,3,4]
  • "Physiotherapy or another treatment beats waiting at one year" — C.[2,3,4,5]

What this means in practice

  • Know the trade-off before you say yes to a shot: faster relief in the first weeks, but a higher chance of the pain coming back and a worse result at a year in the trials.[2,3,4]
  • Time is on your side. In every trial, most people were better at a year — including those who were only told to wait and see.[2,3,4]
  • Physiotherapy is a reasonable option if you want help in the first weeks without the rebound seen after injections, but don't expect it to change the one-year result.[3,4]
  • Get your elbow checked if the pain started with a fall or injury, if your elbow is swollen, locks or won't straighten, or if you have numbness or tingling in your hand — those need an assessment.
