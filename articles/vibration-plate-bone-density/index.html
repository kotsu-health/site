Do vibration plates build bone? What the trials show

Vibration plates are all over social media as a way to strengthen bones after menopause. A surgeon reads the meta-analyses of the randomised trials: what they show for bone density, falls and fractures — and what they don't.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 16 November 2026

The verdict

“Standing on a vibration plate builds stronger bones”

C · Early

Key points

  • Meta-analyses of randomised trials in postmenopausal women disagree: one of 15 trials found no difference in its main analysis, while an update of 20 trials found gains at the lumbar spine with some protocols.
  • Only one protocol had high-quality evidence: high frequency (about 30 Hz), low magnitude (about 0.3 g) and a high cumulative dose (about 7,000 minutes) — for lumbar spine bone density only.
  • Across 30 trials, the overall effect on bone density was small (Hedges' g 0.11).
  • In adults over 50, four trials (746 people) found about a third fewer falls with vibration training (rate ratio 0.67), but only one trial reported fractures, and it found no significant reduction.
  • Vibration is not better than regular exercise: added to an exercise programme it made a negligible difference, and combined aerobic plus resistance training ranked best for spine bone density.

Vibration plates — platforms you stand on while they shake — are everywhere on social media right now, often pitched to women after menopause as an easy way to "build bone" in ten minutes a day. The idea isn't new: researchers have tested whole-body vibration for bone in randomised trials for two decades. So what did they find?

Short version: the bone effect is small and depends heavily on the settings; there is no proof it prevents fractures; and it doesn't beat ordinary exercise.

Bone density: the meta-analyses don't agree

  • A 2016 meta-analysis of 15 randomised trials in postmenopausal women (each lasting at least 6 months) found no differences in its primary analysis. Gains at the lumbar spine and hip appeared only in certain subgroups — for example, after removing low-quality studies, or with particular vibration settings.[1]
  • Its 2023 update included 23 studies (20 in the meta-analysis) and found significant effects on lumbar spine bone density in the main analysis. But only two protocols had high-quality evidence: high frequency (about 30 Hz) with low magnitude (about 0.3 g), and a high cumulative dose (about 7,000 minutes) with low magnitude — and only for the lumbar spine.[2]
  • A broader 2022 meta-analysis of 30 trials found an overall improvement in bone density that was small (Hedges' g 0.11, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.22) — and about the same in postmenopausal women (g 0.09).[3]
  • In women who already have osteoporosis, a 2024 meta-analysis of 13 trials (783 women) found small increases in lumbar spine and femoral neck bone density, and less pain, but no effect on muscle mass.[4]

Seven thousand minutes is roughly 20 minutes a day, every day, for about a year — and even then the high-quality evidence covers the spine, not the hip. That's why "vibration plates build stronger bones" gets a C: there's a signal, but it is small, inconsistent between reviews, and tied to specific settings that a home device may or may not deliver.

Falls and fractures: the outcome that matters

Bone density is a stand-in. What people actually want to avoid is a broken bone.

A meta-analysis of 14 trials in adults aged 50 and over looked at exactly that:[5]

  • Fractures: only one trial reported them, and the reduction was not significant (risk ratio 0.47, 95% CI 0.14 to 1.57).[5]
  • Falls: four trials (746 people) found a lower rate of falls with vibration training (rate ratio 0.67, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.89) — moderate-quality evidence.[5]
  • The share of people who fell at all showed only a trend (risk ratio 0.76, not significant; low-quality evidence).[5]
  • In this review, vibration had no overall effect on bone density.[5]

So "a vibration plate prevents fractures" gets a D — it has not been shown. "Vibration training reduces falls in older adults" gets a B: a moderate-quality signal from a handful of trials, which may work through balance and muscle response rather than bone.[5]

Is it better than exercise?

No.

  • A stimulus-focused meta-analysis found that vibration improved hip and spine bone density compared with no intervention, but when added to an exercise programme the results were negligible compared with exercise alone.[6]
  • A 2025 network meta-analysis of 49 papers (3,360 women) compared exercise types for bone density after menopause. Aerobic plus resistance training had the best effect; vibration was among the options with a significant effect at the femoral neck, but did not top the ranking.[7]

"A vibration plate works better than regular exercise" gets a D.

What these studies cannot tell you

  • Settings vary enormously. Frequency, magnitude, posture (knees bent or straight) and total minutes differ between trials, and the reviews say the ideal protocol is still unclear.[1,2,6]
  • Home devices aren't the trial devices. Most trials used defined platforms and supervised sessions; a consumer plate's actual frequency and magnitude may be unknown.
  • Safety at higher magnitudes (1 g and above) has not been well studied, and the 2023 review flagged it as a concern.[2]
  • No trial was large enough to show a difference in fractures.[5]

Verdict

  • "Vibration plates build stronger bones" — C: small, inconsistent effects, best evidence only at the lumbar spine with specific settings.[1,2,3,4]
  • "A vibration plate prevents fractures" — D: one small trial, no significant effect.[5]
  • "Vibration training reduces falls in older adults" — B: four trials, moderate-quality evidence.[5]
  • "A vibration plate works better than regular exercise" — D.[6,7]

What this means in practice

  • Do this instead (or first): combine resistance training with aerobic exercise — that combination had the strongest effect on spine bone density in postmenopausal women.[7]
  • If you enjoy a vibration plate, treat it as an add-on, not a substitute for exercise or for any bone treatment your doctor has recommended.[6]
  • If you have osteoporosis, a joint replacement, or a heart or balance problem, ask your doctor before using one — the safety of stronger settings is not well established.[2]
  • If you are worried about your bones, ask about a bone density scan and your fracture risk; a plate can't tell you either.
