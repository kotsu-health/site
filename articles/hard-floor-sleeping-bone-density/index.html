Does sleeping on a hard floor increase bone density?

A viewer asked whether sleeping on a hard floor builds stronger bones. No study has tested it. A surgeon reads what has been tested — bone loss during weeks of bed rest, how it recovers, what jump training does for the hip, and a randomised trial of mattress firmness — to grade the claim.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 27 November 2026

The verdict

“Sleeping on a hard floor increases bone density”

D · Unsupported

Key points

  • No published study has compared bone density in people who sleep on a hard floor with people who sleep on a mattress.
  • Lying down is the condition researchers use to cause bone loss: after 17 weeks of continuous bed rest, six healthy men lost 3.6% of bone density at the femoral neck and 3.9% at the lumbar spine.
  • Bone responds to load. A meta-analysis of 18 jump-training trials (666 adults) found femoral neck bone density rose by about 1.5% compared with no jumping.
  • For back pain, the only large randomised trial found the opposite of 'harder is better': 313 adults with chronic low back pain did better on a medium-firm mattress than on a firm one.
  • If the goal is stronger bones, the tested options are loading during the day (impact and resistance exercise), not a harder bed at night.

A viewer asked under one of our videos: does sleeping on hard floors increase bone density? It is a fair question — floor sleeping is common in some cultures and has become a wellness trend elsewhere. Here is what the research can and cannot say.

Has anyone tested it?

No. We searched PubMed for studies comparing bone density in people who sleep on a floor or a firm surface with people who sleep on a mattress, and found none. So any claim that a hard floor builds bone is, at present, untested.

That does not leave us with nothing. Two well-studied questions sit right next to it: what happens to bone when people lie down for long periods, and what kind of load makes bone stronger.

Lying still is how researchers make bone weaker

Bed-rest studies are used by space agencies to mimic weightlessness, precisely because lying down takes load off the skeleton.

  • After 17 weeks of continuous bed rest, six healthy men lost bone at the lumbar spine (3.9%), femoral neck (3.6%), trochanter (4.6%) and heel bone (10.4%).[1]
  • In 60 days of head-down bed rest, 24 healthy women lost bone most at the lower shin and upper thigh bone, and some of those losses were still measurable one year later.[3]

Sleep is not bed rest: we get up every morning and load our bones again. But these studies show the direction of the effect: time spent lying down — on any surface — is time when the skeleton is unloaded. A hard surface does not change that; what bone senses is the force going through it, and a lying body puts very little force through the hips and spine whatever it lies on.

Bone comes back when load comes back

The same research shows that bone responds to being used again.

  • In the 17-week study, the heel bone recovered almost completely within 6 months of walking again, though most other sites recovered more slowly.[1]
  • After 90 days of bed rest in 25 men, losses in the shin bone shaft were fully recovered by 180 days in the groups that had exercise or medication, and the remaining loss at the bone ends was only 0.6% after one year.[2] The authors noted that recovery of muscle and a return to impact sport seemed to come before bone recovery.[2]

Recovery was not complete everywhere in every study, which is why "bone fully recovers" gets a C rather than a B.[3]

What does build bone: load during the day

Jumping is a high-impact loading exercise. A meta-analysis of 18 jump-training trials with 666 adults found femoral neck bone density rose by about 1.5% compared with people who did not jump; the gain was seen in both younger and older adults.[4] The median routine in those trials was 50 jumps, four times a week.[4] These are small gains in bone density, and the trials did not measure fractures. We graded the viral "jump 50 times a day" routine in more detail in Does jumping 50 times a day strengthen your bones?.

And for your back?

Floor sleeping is also promoted for back pain, on the idea that a firmer surface is better. The best test of that idea found the opposite. In a double-blind randomised trial, 313 adults with chronic low back pain were given either a firm or a medium-firm mattress. After 90 days, the medium-firm group had better results for pain in bed, pain on rising and disability.[5] One trial is not the last word, and it compared two mattresses rather than a mattress and a floor, but it does not support "harder is better".

Verdict

  • "Sleeping on a hard floor increases bone density" — D. It has never been tested, and the closest evidence shows that lying down unloads bone rather than building it.[1,2]
  • "Weeks of bed rest cause bone loss" — B: consistent in controlled studies, though the groups were small.[1,3]
  • "Jumping exercise increases hip bone density" — B: a meta-analysis of randomised trials, with modest gains.[4]
  • "A firmer surface is better for back pain" — D in the one large trial that tested it.[5]

What this means in practice

  • Sleep on whatever lets you sleep well; there is no evidence that a harder surface helps your bones.
  • If you want stronger bones, the tested route is load during the day: impact exercise such as jumping, and resistance training.[4]
  • If you have osteoporosis, a previous fracture, or joint or heart problems, check with your doctor before starting high-impact exercise.
