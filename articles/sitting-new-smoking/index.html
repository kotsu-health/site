Is sitting the new smoking? What the numbers actually show

Long sitting is linked to a higher risk of death, but the size of that link is far smaller than smoking's, and enough daily activity appeared to cancel it in a study of over a million people.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 22 October 2026

The verdict

“Sitting is as dangerous as smoking”

D · Unsupported

Evidence at a glance

ClaimGradeRefs
Sitting is as dangerous as smokingD · Unsupported1, 2, 3
More sedentary time is associated with a higher risk of death, heart disease, type 2 diabetes and cancerB · Moderate1
About 60–75 minutes a day of moderate activity is associated with no excess risk of death from long sittingB · Moderate2

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • A meta-analysis of cohort studies linked high sedentary time with about a 24% higher risk of death from any cause, after adjusting for physical activity.
  • In current US smokers, the death rate was about three times that of people who had never smoked, and life expectancy was more than 10 years shorter.
  • In data from over 1 million adults, people who sat more than 8 hours a day but were in the most active group had no clear increase in risk of death.
  • All of this comes from observational studies, so it shows association, not proof of cause.

"Sitting is the new smoking" became a headline because it is memorable. Long sitting really is linked to worse health. But when you put the two sets of numbers side by side, the comparison does not hold up.

How big is the sitting risk?

A systematic review in the Annals of Internal Medicine pooled prospective studies that adjusted for physical activity.[1] High sedentary time was associated with:[1]

Outcome Hazard ratio (high vs low sedentary time)
Death from any cause 1.24
Death from heart disease 1.18
Death from cancer 1.17
Type 2 diabetes 1.91

The links were generally stronger in people who did little physical activity.[1] Sitting time was self-reported in almost all of these studies, and the designs varied widely.[1]

How big is the smoking risk?

In a US survey of over 200,000 adults linked to death records, current smokers aged 25 to 79 had a death rate about three times that of people who had never smoked (hazard ratio 3.0 for women and 2.8 for men).[3] Smokers lost more than 10 years of life expectancy.[3]

A roughly 24% higher risk is a real signal, but it is a different order of magnitude from a threefold one.[1,3]

Activity changes the sitting picture

A harmonised analysis of more than 1 million adults asked whether exercise offsets sitting.[2] Compared with people who sat less than 4 hours a day and were the most active, those who sat more than 8 hours a day but were also in the most active group (about 60–75 minutes of moderate activity a day) had no clear increase in risk of death (hazard ratio 1.04, 95% CI 0.99–1.10).[2]

In contrast, people who sat the least but were the least active had a higher risk (hazard ratio 1.27), and those who sat most and moved least had the highest (1.59).[2] TV time was different: watching 5 or more hours a day was still linked to higher risk even in the most active group.[2]

Why the grades

These are observational studies: people who sit a lot may differ in many other ways, so the sitting findings are graded B.[1,2] Equating sitting with smoking is graded D because the measured risks are far apart.[1,3]

What this means in practice

  • If your job keeps you seated, the larger lever in these data was how much you move overall, not the chair alone.[2]
  • Long evening TV sessions were the one sitting habit that activity did not fully offset.[2]
  • If back or knee pain is what keeps you from moving, it is worth getting assessed — it is often treatable.
