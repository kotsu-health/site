Do cortisone shots help knee arthritis — or wreck the knee?

Steroid injections are one of the most common treatments for an arthritic knee, and one of the most argued about. A surgeon reads the Cochrane review, the two-year injection trials and the large cohort studies on how long the relief lasts and what happens to the cartilage.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 2 November 2026

The verdict

“A cortisone shot gives lasting relief for knee arthritis”

D · Unsupported

Evidence at a glance

ClaimGradeRefs
A cortisone shot gives lasting (6 months or more) relief for knee arthritisD · Unsupported1, 2
A cortisone shot eases knee arthritis pain for the first few weeksC · Early1
Repeated cortisone shots speed up cartilage loss in the kneeC · Early2, 3, 4, 5

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • A Cochrane review of 27 trials (1,767 people) found pain relief that was moderate 1–2 weeks after injection, small at 13 weeks and not detectable at 26 weeks.
  • The reviewers rated all of that evidence low quality: trials were small, results varied widely, and the one trial with the strongest safeguards against bias found no benefit.
  • In a two-year US trial of 140 people, a shot every 3 months caused slightly more cartilage thinning than saline (0.21 vs 0.10 mm) and no better pain.
  • An earlier two-year trial of 68 people found no extra joint space loss with the same schedule, and a cohort study of 980 knees found no faster X-ray progression than with hyaluronic acid shots.
  • So the fear that one shot ruins a knee is not supported, but repeated shots are not a long-term pain plan either.

An arthritic knee flares up, and a steroid ("cortisone") injection is offered. Some people swear by it; others have read that it destroys cartilage. Both questions — how much it helps, and what it does to the joint — have been tested.

How much does a shot help, and for how long?

A Cochrane review pooled 27 randomised trials with 1,767 people that compared steroid injections with a sham injection or no treatment.[1] Overall, pain was lower after the steroid, by about 1 cm on a 10 cm pain scale; roughly 1 in 8 people treated had a benefit they would not otherwise have had.[1]

The timing matters more than the average. The effect was moderate 1–2 weeks after the injection, small to moderate at 4–6 weeks, small at 13 weeks, and there was no evidence of any effect at 26 weeks.[1] Function followed the same pattern, with no evidence of benefit from 13 weeks on.[1]

The reviewers graded all of this evidence as low quality. Most trials were small and at high or unclear risk of bias, results varied widely between trials, larger trials showed smaller effects, and the single trial with adequate safeguards against bias found no benefit at all.[1] Whether the short-term relief is clinically important therefore remains uncertain — which is why the short-term claim is graded C, not B.

Do shots wear away cartilage?

Two randomised trials gave injections of 40 mg triamcinolone every 3 months for 2 years and compared them with saline.

  • In a US trial of 140 people with knee osteoarthritis and signs of joint inflammation on ultrasound, the steroid group lost slightly more cartilage on MRI: 0.21 mm versus 0.10 mm of thickness in the affected compartment.[2] Pain was no better than with saline.[2] What a difference of about a tenth of a millimetre means for the knee in the long run is not known; the trial notes that a clinically important change in cartilage volume has not been defined.[2]
  • An earlier Canadian trial of 68 people found no difference in joint space loss on X-ray between steroid and saline over 2 years, and reported better pain and stiffness over the whole period with the steroid.[3]

Observational studies point in different directions. In two large US cohorts (791 people, 980 knees), knees that had steroid injections did not progress faster on X-ray than knees that had hyaluronic acid injections, and the rate of later knee replacement was not higher.[4] In another analysis of the Osteoarthritis Initiative, people who had steroid injections were more likely to have a knee replacement over up to nine years (hazard ratio 1.57).[5] Cohort studies like this cannot separate the effect of the injection from the fact that people with worse knees are the ones who get injected, so they cannot show cause and effect.

Taken together, the claim that repeated shots speed up cartilage loss is graded C: one trial found a small extra loss, one trial and one cohort found none, and the clinical meaning of the difference is unclear.[2,3,4,5]

Verdict

  • "A cortisone shot gives lasting relief for knee arthritis" — D. In the pooled trials there was no evidence of an effect at 26 weeks, and two years of shots every 3 months gave no better pain than saline in the most recent trial.[1,2]
  • "A shot eases pain for the first few weeks" — C: a benefit appears at 1–6 weeks, but the evidence is low quality and the best-designed trial found none.[1]
  • "Repeated shots speed up cartilage loss" — C: mixed results; a small extra thinning in one trial, none in another.[2,3,4,5]

What this means in practice

  • A steroid injection is, at best, short-term relief. Expect any benefit to fade over weeks to a few months rather than to last.[1]
  • A single injection is unlikely to "ruin" a knee on current evidence, but a long series of injections as a substitute for a long-term plan is not supported: in the two-year trial, regular shots gave no better pain than saline.[2]
  • These trials studied knee osteoarthritis. A sudden hot, swollen knee, a knee that locks or gives way, or pain after an injury needs a medical assessment rather than an injection by default.
