Does a torn ACL always need surgery? What the randomised trials found

An ACL tear is often assumed to mean a reconstruction. A surgeon reads the two randomised trials that compared early reconstruction with rehabilitation first (and surgery only if needed), and the Cochrane review that graded them.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 4 November 2026

The verdict

“A torn ACL always needs surgery”

D · Unsupported

Key points

  • In a Swedish trial of 121 young, active adults with an acute ACL tear, early reconstruction plus rehabilitation gave no better knee scores at 2 or 5 years than rehabilitation with the option of later surgery.
  • In that trial, 51% of those who started with rehabilitation had a reconstruction within five years; the rest managed without one.
  • In a Dutch trial of 167 people aged 18 to 65, early reconstruction gave a slightly better knee score at two years (5.3 points out of 100), a difference the authors described as of unclear clinical importance; 50% of the rehabilitation group had surgery during follow-up.
  • A Cochrane review rated the evidence as low quality: the trials were small, could not be blinded, and mostly studied young, active adults.
  • A knee that keeps giving way despite good rehabilitation is the usual reason people in these trials went on to surgery.

The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is one of the main ligaments that keeps the knee stable when you twist, cut or land. When it tears — often in football, skiing or netball — many people assume the next step is a reconstruction. That is one reasonable path. But two randomised trials have tested a second path, rehabilitation first with surgery kept in reserve, and the results are worth knowing before you decide.

The Swedish trial: early surgery versus rehabilitation first

The KANON trial took 121 young, active adults with an acute ACL tear in a previously uninjured knee.[1] Everyone received the same structured rehabilitation. Half were also assigned to early reconstruction; the other half had the option of a delayed reconstruction later if they needed it.[1]

At two years, knee scores (the average of four KOOS subscales: pain, symptoms, sport and recreation, and knee-related quality of life, 0 to 100) had improved by 39.2 points with early reconstruction and 39.4 points with rehabilitation first — a difference of 0.2 points.[1] There were no significant differences in the secondary outcomes either.[1] By then, 23 of the 59 people in the rehabilitation group had chosen a delayed reconstruction.[1]

At five years the picture was the same: improvements of 42.9 and 44.9 points, with no significant differences in any KOOS subscale, activity level, meniscus surgery or new osteoarthritis on X-ray.[2] By then, 30 of the 59 (51%) assigned to rehabilitation first had had a reconstruction — so about half had not.[2] Results did not differ between knees reconstructed early, reconstructed late, or treated with rehabilitation alone.[2]

The Dutch trial: a small edge for early surgery

The COMPARE trial in the Netherlands randomised 167 people aged 18 to 65 (mean age 31) to early reconstruction or to rehabilitation with optional delayed reconstruction after three months.[4] Half (41 people, 50%) of the rehabilitation group had a reconstruction during the two years.[4]

The rehabilitation group scored better at three months; from nine months the early-surgery group pulled ahead.[4] At two years the early-surgery group's IKDC knee score (0 to 100) was 5.3 points higher — statistically significant, but the authors wrote that its clinical importance is unclear.[4] They also stressed that half of those randomised to rehabilitation did not need a reconstruction.[4]

How strong is this evidence?

The 2016 Cochrane review found only the Swedish trial at that time and rated the overall evidence as low quality: the trial was small, nobody could be blinded to whether they had surgery, and participants were young, active adults.[3] Return to the same or a higher level of sport looked somewhat more common after reconstruction, but the confidence interval was wide enough to include the opposite.[3] Serious events in the surgery group were mostly surgery-related; in the rehabilitation group they were mostly episodes of the knee giving way.[3]

So the honest summary is: two randomised trials point the same way, but they are small and do not cover every group of patients.

What these trials do not cover

These trials studied adults.[1,4] They do not answer questions about children and teenagers, elite athletes aiming to return to pivoting sport quickly, or knees with other major damage at the same time. Those situations need their own assessment.

Verdict

  • "A torn ACL always needs surgery" — D. In both randomised trials, about half of those who started with rehabilitation never had a reconstruction, and their knee scores were similar.[1,2,4]
  • "Rehabilitation first gives similar knee function to early reconstruction at 2–5 years" — B: two randomised trials agree, but the evidence is limited and was rated low quality.[1,2,3,4]
  • "About half of those who start with rehabilitation later choose reconstruction" — B.[2,3,4]
  • "Early reconstruction gives a clinically important advantage" — C: one trial found a small difference whose clinical importance is unclear.[3,4]

What this means in practice

  • An ACL tear is not automatically a decision for surgery. For many adults, a structured, supervised rehabilitation programme is a reasonable first step, with reconstruction kept as an option.[2,4]
  • The main reason people in these trials later chose surgery was a knee that kept giving way.[3] If that happens despite good rehabilitation, it is worth discussing reconstruction.
  • A swollen knee after a twisting injury, a knee that locks, or one that gives way repeatedly should be examined properly — the right plan depends on your age, your sport and what else is injured.
