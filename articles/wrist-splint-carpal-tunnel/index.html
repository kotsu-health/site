Does a wrist splint fix carpal tunnel syndrome?

A wrist brace at night is often the first thing offered for numb, tingling fingers. A surgeon reads the 2026 trial that tested a rigid splint against a placebo bandage, the Cochrane reviews of splinting and of surgery, and a two-year trial of splints vs steroid injections.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 26 November 2026

The verdict

“A wrist splint fixes carpal tunnel syndrome”

Key points

  • In a 2026 randomised trial of 142 people with carpal tunnel syndrome, symptom scores after 12 weeks did not differ between a rigid wrist splint and a soft placebo bandage that let the wrist move freely.
  • Within a year, 57% of the splint group and 51% of the placebo-bandage group had carpal tunnel surgery.
  • A Cochrane review of 29 trials (1,937 adults) found the evidence insufficient to say whether splinting helps; small benefits were not ruled out, and low-certainty evidence from one trial favoured night-time splints over no treatment.
  • A Cochrane review of surgery vs non-surgical treatment found surgery probably gives a higher rate of clinical improvement than a splint in the long term, but differences in symptom and hand-function scores were smaller than what patients notice as important.
  • Constant numbness, weakness or wasting of the muscles at the base of the thumb are reasons to get the hand assessed rather than keep waiting.

Numb, tingling thumb and fingers, often worse at night: carpal tunnel syndrome happens when the median nerve is squeezed where it passes through the wrist. The first thing many people are handed is a wrist splint — a brace that holds the wrist straight, usually worn at night.

It sounds sensible: keep the wrist neutral and the pressure on the nerve should ease. But until recently, splints had never been tested against a convincing placebo. In 2026, one was.

The trial that tested a splint against a placebo bandage

  • In a Swedish trial, 142 people with carpal tunnel syndrome (symptoms for at least a month, no previous treatment) were randomly assigned to a rigid wrist splint or a soft placebo bandage that allowed full wrist movement, for 6 weeks — extended to 10 weeks if symptoms persisted. If symptoms were still there at 12 weeks, surgery was considered.[1]
  • Symptom scores (1 to 5, higher is worse) improved a little in both groups: from 2.81 to 2.45 with the splint and from 2.80 to 2.52 with the placebo bandage.[1]
  • The difference between the groups was 0.08 points and not statistically significant — far below the 0.9 points the authors took as the smallest change that matters to patients.[1]
  • Within one year, 40 of 70 (57%) in the splint group and 37 of 72 (51%) in the placebo-bandage group had surgery.[1]
  • Minor local side effects were reported by 12 people in each group; there were no serious adverse events.[1]

So holding the wrist rigid did no better than a bandage that didn't hold it at all.[1]

What the Cochrane review of splinting found

The 2026 trial fits what a Cochrane review had already found from older, less rigorous trials.

  • The review included 29 randomised trials with 1,937 adults. All were unblinded, and most did not report clearly how participants were randomised.[2]
  • Compared with no active treatment, splinting may provide little or no benefit in symptoms in the short term (under 3 months): 0.37 points better on the same kind of 1–5 symptom scale, where 1 point is the smallest important difference (low-certainty evidence).[2]
  • When only the trials with adequate randomisation were kept, the difference was essentially zero (0.01 points).[2]
  • Whether a splint is best worn at night only or full time, and for how long, is unclear; low-certainty evidence suggested six months of splinting may be better than six weeks.[2]
  • In one trial of 80 people, night-time splints gave a higher rate of overall improvement than no treatment (low-certainty evidence).[2]
  • The authors concluded there is insufficient evidence to say whether splinting benefits people with carpal tunnel syndrome. Small improvements are not excluded, and because a splint is inexpensive with no plausible long-term harm, small effects could justify trying one — especially for people who don't want injections or surgery.[2]

So "a wrist splint fixes carpal tunnel" gets a D, and "a night splint helps a little" gets a C: possible, small, and uncertain.[1,2]

Is surgery better than a splint?

Carpal tunnel release cuts the ligament that forms the roof of the tunnel, giving the nerve more room. A 2024 Cochrane review pooled 14 trials with 1,231 participants comparing surgery with non-surgical treatments.[3]

  • In the long term (over 3 months), surgery probably gives a higher rate of clinical improvement than a splint (risk ratio 2.10; 3 trials, 210 people; moderate-certainty evidence).[3]
  • But on symptom and hand-function scores, surgery was only 0.26 and 0.36 points better — below the 1 and 0.7 points taken as clinically important differences (moderate-certainty evidence).[3]
  • In the splint groups, 41 of 93 people (44%) were referred for surgery later; none of 83 people in the surgery groups had a repeat operation.[3]
  • Side effects were reported in 61% after surgery and 41% with splints, but the certainty of that evidence was very low.[3]
  • The review authors wrote that the efficacy of surgery is currently unclear, and that the decision should weigh small benefits against the risks: people with severe symptoms may choose surgery, while those with tolerable symptoms can start without it and have surgery only if needed.[3]

So "surgery works better than a splint in the long run" gets a C: more people improve, but the size of the difference on symptom scores is small.[3]

Splint or steroid injection?

The INSTinCTS trial in UK primary care randomised 234 adults with mild-to-moderate carpal tunnel syndrome to one steroid injection or a night splint worn for 6 weeks.[4]

  • At 12 and 24 months, there were no statistically significant differences in symptom scores or hand and wrist pain between the groups.[4]
  • By 24 months, 22% of the injection group and 16% of the night-splint group had had carpal tunnel surgery.[4]

So "a steroid injection beats a night splint over two years" gets a D — in this trial, neither came out ahead in the long run.[4]

What these studies cannot tell you

  • Most participants had mild-to-moderate symptoms. The splint trials tell us little about severe carpal tunnel syndrome with constant numbness or muscle wasting.[1,2,4]
  • Splint trials can't be fully blinded. People know whether they are wearing a brace; the 2026 trial dealt with this by using a placebo bandage.[1,2]
  • Symptom scores are averages. Some people improve a lot and some not at all; the trials can't say in advance who will.

Verdict

  • "A wrist splint fixes carpal tunnel syndrome" — D: no better than a placebo bandage in a 2026 trial, and more than half had surgery within a year anyway.[1,2]
  • "A night splint helps a little" — C: possible small benefit, low-certainty evidence.[2]
  • "Surgery works better than a splint in the long run" — C: a higher rate of improvement, but small differences on symptom scores.[3]
  • "A steroid injection beats a night splint over two years" — D.[4]

What this means in practice

  • A splint is a reasonable, low-risk thing to try — not a cure. The Cochrane authors describe it as inexpensive with no plausible long-term harm, but in the best trial it did no better than a placebo bandage. Whether wearing it longer helps more is uncertain.[1,2]
  • Surgery is a choice about severity and preference, made with your doctor, not an automatic next step — and not a failure if you choose it.[3]
  • Get your hand assessed if numbness is constant, if you are dropping things, or if the muscles at the base of your thumb look thinner — these are not wait-and-see signs.
