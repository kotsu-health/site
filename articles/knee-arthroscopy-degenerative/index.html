Does keyhole surgery help a worn-out knee? What the sham-surgery trials found

Arthroscopic 'clean-ups' and partial meniscectomy were among the most common knee operations for middle-aged and older adults. A surgeon reads the placebo-controlled trials, the head-to-head trials against exercise therapy, and the meta-analysis of benefits and harms.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 1 November 2026

The verdict

“Arthroscopic surgery gives lasting relief for a degenerative (worn) knee”

D · Unsupported

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • In a US trial of 180 people with knee osteoarthritis, arthroscopic débridement or lavage gave no better pain or function at any point over two years than a placebo operation with skin incisions only.
  • In a Finnish trial of 146 people aged 35–65 with a degenerative meniscus tear and no osteoarthritis, partial meniscectomy was no better at 12 months than sham surgery; both groups improved by similar amounts.
  • Trials comparing surgery with supervised exercise or physical therapy found no clinically relevant difference in function at 6 months to 2 years.
  • A meta-analysis of nine trials found a small pain benefit of about 2.4 mm on a 100 mm scale that faded after six months, no benefit for function, and harms including blood clots.
  • These findings are about degenerative knees in middle-aged and older adults; they do not answer questions about acute injuries in younger people.

Your knee has ached for months, an MRI shows a "torn meniscus" or "wear and tear", and someone suggests a quick keyhole operation to tidy it up. It sounds logical: trim the frayed edges, wash out the debris, and the pain should settle. This is one of the few operations that has been tested against a fake (sham) operation — and it did not beat it.

Clean-up surgery versus a placebo operation

In a US trial, 180 people with knee osteoarthritis were randomly assigned to arthroscopic débridement, arthroscopic lavage (washing out the joint), or a placebo procedure: skin incisions and a simulated operation without the arthroscope going into the knee.[1] Neither the patients nor the people measuring outcomes knew who had which.[1] At no point over two years did either real operation give less pain or better function than the placebo, and the confidence intervals excluded any clinically meaningful difference.[1]

A Canadian trial asked a different question: does adding arthroscopic surgery to good physical and medical therapy help people with moderate-to-severe knee osteoarthritis?[3] At two years, total WOMAC scores were 874 with surgery and 897 without (a scale up to 2,400, higher is worse) — no meaningful difference.[3]

The torn meniscus seen on MRI

Many middle-aged knees have a degenerative meniscus tear — one that develops gradually rather than from a clear injury. The Finnish FIDELITY trial took 146 people aged 35 to 65 with symptoms of a degenerative medial meniscus tear and no knee osteoarthritis, and randomly assigned them to partial meniscectomy or sham surgery.[2] At 12 months both groups had improved by similar amounts: the Lysholm knee score rose by 21.7 points after real surgery and 23.3 after sham, and pain after exercise fell by 3.1 and 3.3 points.[2] There was no difference in the number needing further knee surgery.[2]

Surgery versus exercise therapy

Two large trials compared the operation with a structured programme of physical therapy or exercise:

  • In the US METEOR trial (351 people aged 45 or older with a meniscal tear and mild-to-moderate osteoarthritis), function improved by 20.9 points with surgery and 18.5 points with physical therapy at 6 months — not a significant difference.[5] About 30% of the physical-therapy group had crossed over to surgery by then, which matters when reading the result.[5]
  • In a Norwegian trial of 140 people with a mean age of 49.5, 12 weeks of supervised exercise and partial meniscectomy gave no clinically relevant difference in knee scores at two years, and thigh strength improved more with exercise at three months.[6] 19% of the exercise group later had surgery, with no extra benefit.[6]

A 2020 meta-analysis of five randomised trials in knees with mild or no osteoarthritis found that partial meniscectomy did not improve function or reduce pain compared with conservative treatment or sham surgery.[7]

Benefits and harms together

A 2015 BMJ meta-analysis pooled nine trials of arthroscopic surgery (partial meniscectomy, débridement or both) in middle-aged and older people with knee pain.[4] The pain benefit was small — about 2.4 mm on a 0–100 mm scale — seen at three and six months and gone by one to two years.[4] There was no significant benefit for physical function.[4] On the other side of the ledger, harms included symptomatic deep vein thrombosis (about 4 per 1,000 procedures), pulmonary embolism, infection and death.[4] The authors concluded that the evidence does not support arthroscopic surgery for these patients.[4]

What these trials do not cover

The trials studied degenerative knees in middle-aged and older adults.[1,2,4] They do not answer whether arthroscopy helps after an acute injury in a younger, active person, or for a knee that genuinely locks. Those are separate questions and need their own assessment.

Verdict

  • "Arthroscopic surgery gives lasting relief for a degenerative knee" — D. Placebo-controlled trials and a meta-analysis found no lasting benefit.[1,2,3,4]
  • "Partial meniscectomy for a degenerative tear beats a sham operation" — D.[2]
  • "Supervised exercise or physical therapy gives similar function to surgery for a degenerative tear" — A: several randomised trials and a meta-analysis agree.[5,6,7]
  • "Knee arthroscopy has real, if uncommon, harms" — B: from pooled cohort and register data.[4]

What this means in practice

  • A degenerative meniscus tear on MRI in a middle-aged knee is common and, on its own, is not a reason for surgery: in trials, structured exercise did as well as the operation.[5,6,7]
  • If you are offered a knee "clean-up" for wear-related pain, it is reasonable to ask whether a supervised exercise programme has been tried first.[4,6]
  • A knee that suddenly locks, gives way after an injury, or swells markedly needs a proper examination — the trials above were not about those situations.
