Your MRI shows a bulging disc. Is that why your back hurts?

Disc bulges and 'degeneration' are common on the scans of people with no back pain at all. A surgeon reads the evidence on what spine MRI findings do and do not explain — and when a scan helps.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 29 October 2026

The verdict

“A bulging disc on your MRI explains your back pain”

C · Early

Evidence at a glance

ClaimGradeRefs
Disc bulges and degeneration are common on scans of people with no back painA · Strong1
A bulging disc on your MRI explains your back painC · Early1, 2
Getting a scan straight away improves outcomes in back pain without warning signsD · Unsupported3
An early MRI without a clear reason leads to worse outcomesC · Early4

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • In a systematic review of 3,110 people with no back pain, disc bulges were found in 30% of 20-year-olds and 84% of 80-year-olds.
  • Disc degeneration on scans rose from 37% at age 20 to 96% at age 80 in the same pain-free people — much of it looks like normal ageing.
  • In adults aged 50 or younger, several findings, including disc bulge, were more common in people with back pain than without, so findings are not meaningless — but they are common in both groups.
  • In trials of back pain without signs of a serious cause, getting a scan straight away did not improve pain or function compared with usual care.
  • Evidence that a specific finding is the cause of one person's pain is limited; a scan has to be read together with symptoms and examination.

You have back pain, you get an MRI, and the report says "disc bulge" or "degenerative disc disease". It sounds like the answer. The question is how often the same words would appear on the scan of someone your age who has no pain at all.

What scans of pain-free people show

Researchers at the Mayo Clinic pooled 33 studies that scanned the spines of 3,110 people with no back pain.[1] The findings were common at every age, and they rose steadily with age:[1]

  • Disc degeneration: 37% of 20-year-olds, rising to 96% of 80-year-olds.
  • Disc bulge: 30% at age 20, rising to 84% at age 80.
  • Disc protrusion: 29% at age 20, rising to 43% at age 80.
  • Annular fissure (a small tear in the outer disc wall): 19% at age 20, rising to 29% at age 80.

The authors concluded that many of these features are likely part of normal ageing and not linked to pain, and that they must be interpreted alongside a person's symptoms.[1]

So are the findings meaningless? Not quite

The same group then compared people aged 50 or younger with and without back pain, in 14 studies of 3,097 people.[2] Several findings were more common in those with pain, including disc bulge, disc extrusion, disc protrusion, disc degeneration, Modic type 1 changes and spondylolysis (a stress defect in the vertebra).[2] Others, such as annular fissures and spondylolisthesis, were not significantly linked to pain.[2]

That is an association across groups. It does not tell you that the bulge on your scan is the source of your pain, because the same finding is also common in people who feel fine.[1,2] The confidence intervals for some findings were also very wide, which means the size of the link is uncertain.[2] This is why the claim is graded C, not D: the findings are not irrelevant, but on their own they rarely explain the pain.

Does getting a scan early help?

A Lancet meta-analysis of 6 randomised trials (1,804 people) compared immediate imaging — X-ray, MRI or CT — with usual care without immediate imaging, in back pain with no signs of a serious underlying condition.[3] There was no meaningful difference in pain or function in the short term (up to 3 months) or the long term (6–12 months).[3] The authors advised against routine immediate imaging in that situation.[3]

There may also be a downside. In a 2-year study of 3,264 workers' compensation cases with acute back pain, people who had an MRI in the first 30 days without a clear reason for one had worse disability outcomes and more surgery and medical costs.[4] This was an observational study, so it cannot prove that the scan caused the worse outcomes — the evidence is limited.[4]

Verdict

  • Disc bulges and degeneration are common on the scans of pain-free people — A.[1]
  • "A bulging disc on your MRI explains your back pain" — C: findings are more common in people with pain, but too common in people without pain to explain one person's symptoms on their own.[1,2]
  • Getting a scan straight away improves back pain without warning signs — D.[3]

What this means in practice

  • If your report lists "bulge" or "degeneration", read it in context: in the pooled studies, disc bulges showed up in nearly a third of pain-free 20-year-olds and in most pain-free 80-year-olds.[1]
  • A scan is most useful when it might change what happens next — for example when there are warning signs of a serious cause, or symptoms such as leg weakness or numbness that need assessment. Ask your doctor what the scan is meant to decide.
  • If back pain is severe, getting worse, or comes with new weakness, numbness, or problems with bladder or bowel control, see a doctor promptly.
