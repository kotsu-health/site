Should you rest in bed when your back goes out?

Bed rest was standard advice for acute low back pain for decades. A surgeon reads the randomised trials and the Cochrane review on resting versus staying active — and what the evidence says to do instead.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 31 October 2026

The verdict

“Bed rest helps acute low back pain get better”

D · Unsupported

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • In a Cochrane review of randomised trials, people with acute low back pain who were advised to stay active had slightly less pain and slightly better function than those advised to rest in bed.
  • In a Finnish trial of 186 workers with acute back pain, those who carried on with ordinary activities as tolerated recovered faster than those prescribed two days of bed rest; recovery was slowest with bed rest.
  • In a US trial of 203 patients, advising two days of bed rest instead of seven led to 45% fewer missed work days with no difference in other outcomes.
  • For sciatica (pain spreading down the leg), the Cochrane review found little or no difference between bed rest and staying active.
  • The benefits of staying active are small, but there is no sign that bed rest helps; current guidelines advise resuming normal activity.

You bend to pick something up, your lower back seizes, and the instinct is to lie down and wait it out for a few days. For a long time that was also the medical advice. It has been tested in randomised trials, and it does not hold up.

Resting versus staying active: the trials

A Cochrane review pooled randomised trials that compared advice to rest in bed with advice to stay active.[1] For acute low back pain, two trials with 401 people showed small improvements in pain (standardised mean difference 0.22) and in function (0.29) in favour of staying active.[1] The reviewers rated this moderate-quality evidence and described the benefit as small.[1]

One of the clearest single trials comes from Helsinki.[2] City employees with acute, non-specific back pain were randomly assigned to two days of bed rest (67 people), back-mobilising exercises (52), or carrying on with ordinary activities as far as the pain allowed (67).[2] At 3 and 12 weeks, the ordinary-activity group did best on duration and intensity of pain, back flexibility, disability scores and days off work. Recovery was slowest in the bed rest group.[2]

If you do rest, is shorter better?

An earlier US trial of 203 patients with mechanical back pain compared advice to rest in bed for two days with advice to rest for seven.[3] The two-day group missed 45% fewer days of work (3.1 vs 5.6 days), and there was no difference in other functional, physical or self-rated outcomes.[3] Resting longer did not buy a better recovery.

What about sciatica?

Sciatica — pain that spreads down the leg, usually from an irritated nerve root — is a different picture. The Cochrane review found moderate-quality evidence of little or no difference in pain or function between bed rest and staying active.[1] So for sciatica, bed rest does not look helpful either, but there is also no clear evidence that staying active works better. That claim is graded C.

What guidelines say now

A 2018 Lancet series on low back pain summarised clinical guidelines from several countries: they recommend education that supports self-management and a return to normal activities and exercise, with prudent use of medication, imaging and surgery.[4] The authors also noted that rest is still used too often in practice.[4]

Verdict

  • "Bed rest helps acute low back pain get better" — D. In trials, bed rest did no better and sometimes slightly worse than staying active.[1,2,3]
  • Advice to stay active gives slightly better pain and function — B: consistent direction, small effect, moderate-quality evidence.[1,2]
  • Seven days of bed rest beats two — D.[3]
  • For sciatica, staying active beats bed rest — C: little or no difference either way.[1]

What this means in practice

  • With an ordinary episode of acute back pain, carry on with your usual activities as far as the pain allows, rather than stopping altogether.[2,4]
  • Bed rest is not a treatment: in the trials it did not speed recovery, and resting for a week instead of two days only meant more time off work.[1,3]
  • The trials studied back pain without signs of a serious cause. If back pain follows a fall or injury, comes with fever, new leg weakness or numbness, or problems with bladder or bowel control, see a doctor promptly.
