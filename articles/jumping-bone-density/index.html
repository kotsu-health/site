Does jumping 50 times a day strengthen your bones?

"Jump 50 times when you wake up" is a viral morning routine. A surgeon reads the meta-analyses of jump-training trials: what jumping does for hip and spine bone density, who was studied, and what has never been tested.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 17 November 2026

The verdict

“Jumping 50 times a day strengthens your bones”

B · Moderate

Key points

  • A 2024 meta-analysis of 18 jump-training trials (666 adults) found a small gain in femoral neck (hip) bone density of about 1.5%, with moderate certainty, at a median dose of 50 jumps four times a week.
  • The hip benefit held in older adults too (about 1%), but gains at the total hip and trochanter were seen only in younger adults.
  • The spine responds less: jumping alone did not significantly raise lumbar spine bone density in several meta-analyses; impact combined with heavy resistance training did.
  • No trial in these reviews measured fractures, so 'jumping prevents fractures' is not proven.
  • We found no trials of the viral morning routine itself (energy, mood, metabolism), so those claims are not graded here.

A morning routine is doing the rounds on social media: jump 50 times as soon as you get out of bed. Fans say it wakes you up, lifts your mood, revs your metabolism — and strengthens your bones.

The energy and mood claims haven't been tested as a routine, as far as we could find. The bone claim has a real research base: jumping is one of the most studied "bone-loading" exercises there is. So here is what the trials actually show.

Short version: jumping gives a small, real gain at the hip, a weaker one at the spine, and no trial has shown it prevents fractures.

The hip: a small gain, with moderate certainty

  • A 2024 meta-analysis pooled 18 jump-training trials (666 adults). Jumping increased femoral neck bone density — the top of the thigh bone, where hip fractures happen — by about 1.5% (95% CI 0.83% to 2.17%) compared with not jumping.[1]
  • The gain held in younger adults (about 1.8%) and in older adults (about 1.0%, 95% CI 0.02% to 2.03%).[1]
  • The median dose in these trials was 50 jumps, four times a week, and the authors judged a femoral neck gain at that dose to be of moderate certainty. More jumps did not clearly mean more benefit.[1]
  • In premenopausal women, a meta-analysis of 6 studies also found gains at the femoral neck and trochanter (both parts of the hip).[2]

So the viral number — 50 jumps — happens to sit right where the research is. That's why "jumping strengthens your hip bones" gets a B: consistent, but small, and measured as bone density rather than broken bones.

One neat trial shows the effect is local. Older men (average age 70) hopped on one leg only, daily, for 12 months; 34 finished, attending 92% of sessions. Cortical bone at the hip increased by 2.7% and the density of the bone's inner layer by 6.4%, in localized regions of the femur — the leg that hopped changed more than the leg that didn't.[6]

The spine: less convincing

  • In the 2024 meta-analysis, lumbar spine bone density did not rise significantly with jump training — in younger or older adults.[1]
  • In premenopausal women, the spine "seemed to benefit less" from jumping.[2] High-impact exercise alone helped only the hip; impact combined with heavy resistance training helped both hip and spine.[3]
  • In older adults, a meta-analysis of 19 randomised trials (1,577 people) found that impact mixed with resistance training improved the spine, and "odd-impact" exercise (jumping in different directions) improved both spine and hip — though results varied a lot between trials.[4]
  • A 2023 meta-analysis of 28 trials (2,985 people) using 3D scans found no effect of impact exercise on lumbar spine density or structure.[5]

"Jumping strengthens your spine" gets a C: possible, mainly when jumping is combined with lifting, but not shown for jumping alone.

Fractures: never tested

None of these reviews reported fractures as an outcome; they measured bone density and bone structure.[1,5] The 2023 review called for more high-quality trials to find the best protocols.[5]

"Jumping 50 times a day prevents broken bones" gets a D — not because it can't help, but because nobody has shown it.

What about people who already have weak bones?

Jumping has traditionally been discouraged in osteoporosis for fear of causing a fracture. The LIFTMOR trial tested that fear: 101 postmenopausal women (average age 65) with low bone mass did 8 months of twice-weekly, 30-minute, supervised high-intensity resistance and impact training — or a low-intensity home programme.[7]

  • Spine bone density rose 2.9% in the training group versus a 1.2% fall in the comparison group; at the femoral neck it was +0.3% versus −1.9%.[7]
  • Every physical function test improved, and there was one adverse event (a minor back spasm).[7]

The key word is supervised: these women were screened and coached. That supports impact plus strength training after menopause (B), but it isn't evidence for unsupervised jumping on a hard floor if you already have osteoporosis or a past fracture.

Verdict

  • "Jumping strengthens your hip bones" — B: small gain (about 1–2%) at the femoral neck, moderate certainty.[1,2]
  • "Jumping strengthens your spine" — C: weak for jumping alone; better when combined with resistance training.[1,3,4,5]
  • "Jumping 50 times a day prevents broken bones" — D: not tested.[1,5]
  • "Impact plus heavy strength training helps bone after menopause, even with low bone mass" — B: shown under supervision.[3,7]
  • Morning energy, mood and metabolism — not graded: we found no trials of this routine.

What this means in practice

  • If you're healthy and your joints are happy, a few dozen jumps on most days is a cheap, research-backed way to load your hips. The trials' median was 50 jumps, four times a week; the time of day doesn't matter.[1]
  • Do this too: add strength training. Impact plus resistance work is what moved the spine as well as the hip.[3,4,7]
  • If you have osteoporosis, a previous fracture, a joint replacement, or painful knees or hips, talk to your doctor or physio first. The evidence for impact training with low bone mass comes from screened, supervised programmes.[7]
  • A small rise in bone density is not the same as fewer fractures. If you're worried about your bones, ask about a bone density scan and your fracture risk.
