Do gel (hyaluronic acid) injections help knee arthritis?

Hyaluronic acid 'gel' or 'lubricant' injections are widely offered for knee arthritis. A surgeon reads the two largest meta-analyses of the placebo-controlled trials — 169 trials and over 21,000 people — and what they found about pain relief and side effects.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 5 November 2026

The verdict

“Gel (hyaluronic acid) injections give meaningful pain relief for knee arthritis”

D · Unsupported

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • A 2022 BMJ meta-analysis pooled 169 randomised trials with 21,163 people comparing hyaluronic acid injections with placebo or no treatment.
  • In the 24 large placebo-controlled trials (8,997 people), the injections reduced pain by about 2 mm on a 100 mm scale — less than the smallest difference patients notice.
  • Smaller trials and published trials showed bigger benefits than large and unpublished ones, a pattern that suggests the early positive results were inflated.
  • In large trials, serious adverse events were more common after hyaluronic acid than after placebo (relative risk 1.49 in 2022; 1.41 in an earlier 2012 review).
  • Guidelines disagree: the 2019 OARSI guideline still lists hyaluronic acid as a conditional option for knee arthritis, while exercise and education are its core treatments.

"Your knee has lost its lubrication — a course of gel injections will top it up." Hyaluronic acid injections, also called viscosupplementation or "gel shots", are one of the most widely used treatments for knee osteoarthritis. The idea is easy to picture. The question is whether the injections relieve pain better than a placebo injection — and that has been tested in a very large number of trials.

What the biggest review found

A 2022 systematic review in The BMJ collected every randomised trial it could find comparing hyaluronic acid with placebo or no treatment: 169 trials with 21,163 participants.[1] Because small trials can exaggerate effects, the authors decided in advance that their main analysis would use only large placebo-controlled trials with at least 100 people per group.[1]

In those 24 large trials (8,997 people), hyaluronic acid reduced pain slightly more than placebo — by about 2 mm on a 100 mm pain scale.[1] The authors had set the smallest difference that matters to patients in advance, and the result was clearly below it.[1] Function showed the same picture.[1] Their cumulative analysis concluded that, since 2009, the evidence has been conclusive that the injections and placebo are clinically equivalent for pain.[1]

Why some people remember it working

Two things explain much of the gap between reputation and results.

  • Small and published trials looked better. The 2022 review found clear signs of small-study effects and publication bias.[1] An earlier review of 89 trials (12,667 adults) found the same: across 71 trials the benefit looked moderate, but in 18 large trials with blinded assessment it shrank to a clinically irrelevant size, and five unpublished trials showed almost no effect at all.[2]
  • Placebo injections help too. In these trials both groups receive an injection, so a knee that feels better after a gel shot is not, on its own, evidence that the gel did the work — the comparison with placebo is what counts.[1]

Side effects

In 15 large placebo-controlled trials (6,462 people), serious adverse events were more common with hyaluronic acid than with placebo (relative risk 1.49).[1] The 2012 review found a similar increase (relative risk 1.41, from 14 trials) and noted that many trials did not report safety data well.[2] The absolute numbers are small, and the reasons for the difference are not clear, which is why this is graded B rather than A.

Guidelines do not all agree

The 2019 OARSI guideline, which pre-dates the 2022 review, still lists intra-articular hyaluronic acid as a conditional option for knee osteoarthritis, depending on a person's other health conditions; it does not recommend it for hip osteoarthritis.[3] The same guideline names arthritis education and structured land-based exercise, with weight management where relevant, as the core treatments for knee osteoarthritis.[3] The 2022 review's authors concluded that their findings do not support broad use of viscosupplementation.[1]

Verdict

  • "Gel injections give meaningful pain relief for knee arthritis" — D. In large placebo-controlled trials the difference was about 2 points out of 100, below what patients notice.[1,2]
  • "Any benefit over placebo is small and below the clinically important difference" — A: two large meta-analyses of randomised trials agree.[1,2]
  • "The injections carry more serious adverse events than placebo" — B: seen in both reviews, but the events are uncommon and poorly reported.[1,2]
  • "Exercise and education are the core first-line treatments" — A.[3]

What this means in practice

  • If you are offered a course of gel injections for knee arthritis, it is reasonable to ask what benefit to expect over a placebo injection — in the large trials, very little.[1]
  • The treatments with the strongest support are structured exercise, education and, where relevant, weight management.[3] These work best as a steady programme, not a one-off.
  • Knee arthritis that keeps limiting your walking or sleep despite a good programme deserves an in-person assessment: the options depend on your X-rays, your symptoms and your goals.
