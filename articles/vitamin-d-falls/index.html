Does vitamin D prevent falls in older adults? What the trials found

Vitamin D is often recommended to keep older people on their feet. A surgeon reads the meta-analyses of falls trials — and the one trial where a big yearly dose made falls more common.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 24 October 2026

The verdict

“Vitamin D supplements prevent falls in older adults living at home”

D · Unsupported

Evidence at a glance

ClaimGradeRefs
Vitamin D supplements prevent falls in older adults living at homeD · Unsupported1, 2
Daily vitamin D (800–1,000 IU) lowers fall risk in older adults with low vitamin D levelsC · Early3, 4
A very large once-a-year dose of vitamin D increases falls in older womenB · Moderate5
Exercise programmes reduce falls in older adults living at homeA · Strong6

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • A meta-analysis of 37 randomised trials (34,144 people) found that vitamin D did not reduce the number of people who fell.
  • A 2025 meta-analysis limited to adults aged 65 and over living at home (10 trials, 23,211 people) also found no effect on fall risk.
  • One network meta-analysis found fewer falls with daily doses of 800–1,000 IU, mainly in people with low vitamin D levels — a subgroup signal, so the evidence is limited.
  • In a trial of 2,256 older women, a single yearly dose of 500,000 IU increased falls and fractures.
  • Exercise programmes have the most consistent evidence for preventing falls in older adults living at home.

Falls are one of the main reasons older people break a hip or a wrist, and vitamin D is often suggested as a way to keep muscles strong and prevent them. Falls are easy to count, so this claim has been tested directly in many randomised trials.

The big picture: dozens of trials

A systematic review in The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology pooled the randomised trials of vitamin D supplements in adults.[1]

Outcome Trials (people) Relative risk with vitamin D (95% CI)
At least one fall 37 (34,144) 0.97 (0.93 to 1.02)
Any fracture 36 (44,790) 1.00 (0.93 to 1.07)
Hip fracture 20 (36,655) 1.11 (0.97 to 1.26)

Results were similar for high versus low doses and for doses above 800 IU a day. A trial sequential analysis suggested that more trials of the same kind are unlikely to show vitamin D reducing falls by 7.5% or more.[1] The authors concluded there is little justification for vitamin D supplements to maintain or improve musculoskeletal health.[1]

Older adults living at home

Earlier analyses mixed care-home residents, hospital patients and people living independently. A 2025 meta-analysis kept only trials of adults aged 65 and over living in the community: 10 trials with 23,211 people.[2]

  • Overall, vitamin D was not associated with fewer falls (odds ratio 0.99, 95% CI 0.95 to 1.03).[2]
  • There was no effect in women or men analysed separately, at doses up to or above 1,000 IU a day, with daily or intermittent dosing, or with shorter or longer treatment.[2]

Where a benefit might exist

Not every analysis is negative. A network meta-analysis of 35 trials (58,937 people) found a lower risk of falls with 800–1,000 IU a day compared with placebo (relative risk 0.85), and a 22% lower risk when that dose was taken daily.[3] The benefit was seen in people with blood vitamin D (25-hydroxyvitamin D) at or below 50 nmol/L (relative risk 0.69), but not in those above it.[3] In the same analysis, doses above 1,000 IU a day were associated with more falls than 800–1,000 IU.[3]

In care homes, a Cochrane review found that vitamin D probably reduces the rate of falls (rate ratio 0.63) but probably makes little or no difference to the number of residents who fall (risk ratio 0.99); the residents in those trials had low vitamin D levels.[4]

These are subgroup and setting-specific findings, so the evidence that vitamin D helps people who are low in it is limited. That is why it is graded C.

When more was worse

One trial tried to make vitamin D easier to take: 2,256 women aged 70 or over, at high risk of fracture, received a single 500,000 IU dose or placebo every autumn or winter for 3 to 5 years.[5] Women given vitamin D had more falls (rate ratio 1.15, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.30) and more fractures (rate ratio 1.26, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.59). The excess in falls was greatest in the first 3 months after each dose.[5]

What does prevent falls

For older adults living at home, the US Preventive Services Task Force evidence review found that exercise programmes were associated with fewer falls (incidence rate ratio 0.85), fewer people falling (relative risk 0.92) and fewer injurious falls (incidence rate ratio 0.84). Exercise showed the most consistent benefit across fall outcomes.[6]

Verdict

For older adults living independently, vitamin D supplements have not been shown to prevent falls: the largest meta-analyses, including one limited to community-dwelling people over 65, found no effect.[1,2] Graded D. People with low vitamin D levels may be an exception, but that evidence is limited.[3]

What this means in practice

  • A vitamin D tablet is not a fall-prevention plan. Regular exercise that includes balance and strength work has the strongest evidence.[6]
  • If you have been told your vitamin D is low, or you take vitamin D as part of osteoporosis treatment, keep following your doctor's advice — do not stop on your own.
  • Avoid very large single doses unless a doctor has prescribed them for a specific reason.[5]
  • If you have fallen, or feel unsteady, ask a doctor for a falls assessment: medicines, eyesight and home hazards can all be checked.
