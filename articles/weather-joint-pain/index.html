Can your joints really predict the rain?

Many people with arthritis say they can feel a change in the weather. A surgeon reads the large studies on rain, cold, humidity and air pressure — and what they do and do not show about joint and back pain.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 30 October 2026

The verdict

“Changes in the weather make joint pain worse”

C · Early

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • In 11.7 million Medicare outpatient visits by adults aged 65 and over, visits for joint or back pain were not more common on rainy days.
  • In an Australian study of people with knee osteoarthritis, temperature, humidity, air pressure and rain were not linked to pain flare-ups.
  • A UK smartphone study of 2,658 people with chronic pain found modest links between pain and humidity, air pressure and wind speed.
  • A review of 43 studies found results pointing in different directions, mostly from small, short studies — so the evidence is limited and inconsistent.
  • Group averages may hide individuals: in rheumatoid arthritis, a minority of patients' pain did seem to follow the weather.

"My knee always knows when rain is coming." It is one of the most common things people with arthritis say. The question is whether large studies, which compare the same people or places on different days, find the same thing.

Rain and 11 million doctor visits

Researchers at Harvard linked US Medicare claims to local rainfall records: 1,552,842 adults aged 65 and over, with 11,673,392 outpatient visits between 2008 and 2012.[1] They compared how often visits were for joint or back pain on rainy days versus dry days in the same area.[1]

After adjustment, 6.35% of visits on rainy days were for joint or back pain, against 6.39% on dry days — a difference so small it is unlikely to matter, and in the opposite direction to the belief.[1] There was also no link with the number of rainy days in the week before a visit, and none in people with rheumatoid arthritis.[1] The authors note that doctor visits are not the same as pain, so a link may still exist.[1]

Knee arthritis and back pain flare-ups

In Australia, people with knee osteoarthritis logged on whenever their knee pain flared, and the weather on those days was compared with the weather on control days.[2] Among 171 people who had at least one flare (1,425 observations), temperature, relative humidity, air pressure and rain showed no apparent link with flare-ups.[2]

A similar design in 993 people with a sudden episode of low back pain found no link with temperature, humidity, air pressure or rain.[4] Higher wind speed and gusts slightly raised the odds of an episode, but the authors judged the increase not clinically important.[4]

But a large smartphone study did find a signal

In the UK "Cloudy with a Chance of Pain" study, 2,658 people with chronic pain recorded their symptoms daily on a smartphone app over 15 months.[3] The researchers found significant but modest links between pain and relative humidity, air pressure and wind speed, which remained after accounting for mood and physical activity.[3]

So the large studies do not agree. A review of 43 studies found that most (67%) reported some link between pain and a weather variable, but for every variable some studies found a link — in either direction — and others found none.[6] Most studies were small and short, and many had a high risk of measuring weather exposure wrongly.[6] This is why the claim is graded C: the evidence is limited and inconsistent.

Averages can hide individuals

A systematic review of 9 studies in rheumatoid arthritis found that, across groups, the links between pain and temperature, humidity or air pressure were close to zero.[5] Yet in two studies that looked person by person, the pain of a minority (fewer than 25%) of patients did seem to be affected by the weather — and different people reacted in different ways.[5] Your experience may be real for you, even if it does not show up as a rule for everyone.

Verdict

  • Rainy days bring more doctor visits for joint or back pain — D.[1]
  • Changes in the weather make joint pain worse — C: some studies find modest links, others find none.[2,3,4,6]
  • Some people's pain follows the weather more than others' — C.[3,5]

What this means in practice

  • Don't let a forecast decide whether you stay active: in these studies, rain and cold did not reliably predict flare-ups.[1,2]
  • If you think the weather affects your pain, a simple daily pain diary is the most honest test — memory tends to remember the rainy days.
  • Pain that is new, severe, keeps getting worse, or comes with swelling, redness or fever needs a doctor's assessment, whatever the weather.
