Does glucosamine rebuild knee cartilage? What the trials actually measured

Glucosamine is sold as a cartilage builder. Large trials that measured the joint space on X-rays found tiny or no differences from placebo. A surgeon explains the numbers.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 18 October 2026

The verdict

“Glucosamine rebuilds or regrows knee cartilage”

D · Unsupported

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • No trial has shown glucosamine regrowing cartilage; trials measure whether the joint space narrows more slowly.
  • A network meta-analysis of 10 large trials (3,803 patients) found the differences in joint space narrowing versus placebo were minute, with uncertainty ranges overlapping zero.
  • In a 2-year trial of 605 people, glucosamine alone did not slow joint space narrowing; the glucosamine–chondroitin combination differed from placebo by 0.10 mm, and no group had less pain than placebo.
  • Pain results differ between reviews and by product: industry-independent trials showed smaller effects than commercially funded ones.

Glucosamine is one of the best-selling joint supplements in the world, and the label often implies it "supports" or "rebuilds" cartilage. In clinic, patients with knee arthritis regularly ask me whether it will grow their cartilage back. Here is what the research can and cannot say.

What the trials can actually measure

Cartilage does not show up on a plain X-ray, but the gap between the bones — the joint space — does. As knee arthritis progresses, that gap narrows. So trials of glucosamine do not test whether cartilage regrows; they test whether the joint space narrows more slowly than with a placebo. No study included here reported cartilage being rebuilt.[1,2]

The biggest independent analysis

A network meta-analysis combined 10 large trials, each with more than 200 patients, covering 3,803 people with hip or knee arthritis.[1]

  • Joint space: the differences between glucosamine, chondroitin or both and placebo were all minute, and every uncertainty range overlapped zero.[1]
  • Pain: on a 10 cm pain scale, glucosamine reduced pain by 0.4 cm compared with placebo. The authors had set 0.9 cm as the smallest difference a patient would notice, and none of the supplements reached it.[1]
  • Who paid for the trial mattered: industry-independent trials showed smaller effects than commercially funded ones.[1]

A two-year trial that measured the X-rays

In an Australian trial, 605 people aged 45–75 with knee pain and early narrowing took glucosamine sulfate, chondroitin, both, or a placebo every day for two years.[2]

  • Glucosamine alone did not change joint space narrowing compared with placebo.[2]
  • The combination narrowed 0.10 mm less than placebo over two years — statistically significant, but a very small amount.[2]
  • Pain fell in all four groups, including placebo, with no difference between them.[2]

Why some reviews sound more positive

Not every review agrees. A 2024 meta-analysis of 25 trials reported a significant reduction in joint space narrowing with glucosamine sulfate,[3] and a review of long-term trials found an association with less pain for glucosamine sulfate, while noting large uncertainty for all treatments compared with placebo.[4] Results depend on which products and trials are included. Even in the more positive analyses, what is measured is slower narrowing — not new cartilage.

That is why "glucosamine rebuilds cartilage" is graded D, while "it slows narrowing" and "it meaningfully relieves pain" are graded C: the evidence is mixed and the effects, where found, are small.

What this means in practice

  • Glucosamine will not regrow cartilage. If a product says it does, the evidence above does not support that.
  • In trials it was generally well tolerated,[3] so some people try it; if you do, judge it honestly after a few months and stop if you notice no difference.
  • Exercise, weight management and strengthening the muscles around the knee are the foundations of care for knee arthritis — talk to a doctor about what fits your knee.
  • Knee pain with swelling, locking or giving way deserves an assessment rather than a supplement.
