Should you rest and ice a sprained ankle? RICE, checked

Rest, ice, compression, elevation: almost everyone knows the RICE rule for a twisted ankle. A surgeon reads the reviews of RICE and ice trials, an overview of 46 systematic reviews, and a meta-analysis of rehabilitation exercise to see what actually helps a sprained ankle recover.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 12 November 2026

The verdict

“Rest and ice are the best treatment for a sprained ankle”

D · Unsupported

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • A meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials (868 patients) found insufficient evidence to determine how well RICE therapy — rest, ice, compression and elevation — works for acute ankle sprains in adults.
  • A systematic review of randomised trials of ice for acute ankle sprain found only two trials, both at high risk of bias; with uncertain evidence, adding ice did not improve swelling, pain or range of motion.
  • An overview of 46 systematic reviews found strong evidence that early mobilisation helps pain, swelling and function after an acute ankle sprain, and moderate evidence for exercise.
  • A meta-analysis of 7 randomised trials (1,417 people) found that exercise-based rehabilitation reduced re-sprains at 7–12 months compared with usual care (odds ratio 0.53).
  • Clinical guidelines still list cryotherapy (ice) among their recommendations, mainly for pain and swelling early on — the evidence is weak, not proof that ice harms.

You roll your ankle on a kerb or in a game. Almost everyone knows what comes next: RICE — rest, ice, compression, elevation. Put your feet up, keep a bag of frozen peas on it, and don't walk on it until it settles. But has anyone checked whether this actually helps the ankle recover?

Is there good evidence for RICE?

A systematic review with meta-analysis looked for randomised trials of RICE, or any of its four parts, started within 72 hours of an acute ankle sprain in adults.[1]

  • After screening, 11 trials with 868 patients were included. The main reason other trials were excluded was that they had no well-defined control group without the treatment being tested.[1]
  • The authors concluded there is insufficient evidence from randomised trials to determine how well RICE works for acute ankle sprains, and that treatment decisions must rest on individual judgement, expert opinion and national guidelines.[1]

That doesn't mean RICE is harmful. It means the package almost everyone learns was never properly tested as a package. That is why "RICE is an effective treatment" gets a C.

What about ice on its own?

A later systematic review searched six databases for randomised trials of ice (cryotherapy) for acute ankle sprain, measuring pain, swelling, range of motion, function and re-injury.[2]

  • Only two trials qualified, and both had a high risk of bias. Both tested ice added to another treatment versus that treatment alone.[2]
  • With uncertain evidence, adding ice did not improve swelling, pain or range of motion.[2]
  • The authors concluded that current studies lack evidence supporting ice for acute ankle sprain, and called for larger, high-quality trials.[2]

At the same time, a review of clinical practice guidelines found that cryotherapy is still among the recommendations guidelines make for ankle sprain.[6] So ice is a reasonable comfort measure many guidelines accept — but there is no good trial evidence that it makes the ankle heal faster. Grade C.

Rest it, or move it?

This is where the evidence is clearer. An overview of 46 systematic reviews on treating and preventing ankle sprains found:[3]

  • Strong evidence for early mobilisation for pain, swelling and function after an acute sprain.[3]
  • Moderate evidence for exercise and manual therapy.[3]
  • Strong evidence for bracing and moderate evidence for neuromuscular (balance) training to prevent another sprain.[3]

A Dutch evidence-based guideline reached the same conclusion: a short period of immobilisation may help pain and swelling, but people with an acute lateral ankle ligament injury benefit most from tape or a brace combined with an exercise programme, and supervised exercise is preferred over passive treatments.[5]

So "rest the ankle until it stops hurting" gets a D, and "move it early and exercise" gets a B — the underlying trials are often small and of mixed quality, which keeps it below an A.

Does exercise stop it happening again?

Ankle sprains often recur. A meta-analysis of 7 randomised trials (1,417 people) compared exercise-based rehabilitation with usual care (some or all of protection, rest, ice, compression and elevation):[4]

  • At 3–6 months there was only a trend toward fewer re-sprains (odds ratio 0.87, 95% CI 0.48 to 1.58).[4]
  • At 7–12 months, re-sprains were significantly reduced with exercise (odds ratio 0.53, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.73).[4]
  • Most programmes focused on balance or strength training, and total training time ranged from 3.5 to 21 hours; the best content and dose are not yet established.[4]

What these studies cannot tell you

  • The RICE and ice trials are few and weak. "Insufficient evidence" means the question is unanswered, not that ice or compression does nothing.[1,2]
  • Most evidence is about lateral (outer) ankle sprains in adults; it doesn't cover fractures, high-ankle sprains or children.
  • "Early mobilisation" in trials usually meant guided, supported movement — not running on an ankle you can't stand on.[3,5]
  • The best exercise programme is unknown. The benefit for re-sprains is clear; the exact exercises and amount are not.[4]

Verdict

  • "Rest and ice are the best treatment for a sprained ankle" — D: the trials don't show rest and ice beat other care, and moving early has stronger evidence.[1,3]
  • "RICE is an effective treatment for an acute ankle sprain" — C: insufficient trial evidence either way.[1]
  • "Ice speeds recovery" — C: only two high-risk-of-bias trials, no added benefit shown; guidelines still list it.[2,6]
  • "Resting until it stops hurting is better than moving early" — D.[3,5]
  • "Early mobilisation and exercise help pain, swelling and function" — B.[3,5]
  • "Exercise-based rehab lowers the risk of a repeat sprain" — B: odds ratio 0.53 at 7–12 months.[4]
  • "A brace or tape helps prevent a repeat sprain" — B.[3,5]

What this means in practice

  • Ice and elevation in the first days are fine for comfort, but don't expect them to speed healing — and don't let them replace movement.[2]
  • Start moving and putting weight on the ankle as pain allows, with tape or a brace for support if needed.[3,5]
  • Once it settles, do balance and strength exercises for the ankle; this is what lowered the risk of spraining it again.[4]
  • Guidelines use the Ottawa ankle rules to decide who needs an X-ray.[6] Get the ankle checked if you can't put weight on it, if there is tenderness over the bone, if it looks deformed, or if it isn't improving after a few days.
