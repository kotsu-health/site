Collagen supplements for skin and joints: what the best trials say

Pooled trials say collagen helps skin, but the independent, high-quality ones don't. For joints, the benefit is short-lived and the evidence weak. A surgeon reads the fine print.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 15 October 2026

The verdict

“Collagen supplements prevent skin aging”

D · Unsupported

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • Meta-analyses that pool every trial report better skin hydration and elasticity with collagen.
  • When only trials without industry funding, or only high-quality trials, are analysed, the skin benefit disappears.
  • For osteoarthritis, collagen hydrolysate showed short-term pain relief in very-low-quality evidence and no clinically important long-term benefit.
  • Skin, tendon and cartilage share collagen as a building block, but swallowing collagen is not the same as building it.

Collagen is the most abundant protein family in the body. It gives skin its firmness, tendons their strength and cartilage its structure. So the pitch for collagen powders is intuitive: replace what age takes away, and skin and joints will follow. The research is more interesting than the pitch.

Skin: a positive headline with a catch

Several meta-analyses have pooled randomized trials of oral hydrolysed collagen. One combined 26 trials with 1,721 participants and found better skin hydration and elasticity than placebo, while noting several sources of bias in the included trials and calling for larger studies.[1] An earlier analysis of 19 trials with 1,125 participants, almost all women, reached a similar conclusion.[2]

In 2025, a meta-analysis of 23 trials with 1,474 participants asked a sharper question: does the result depend on who paid for the study, and on how well it was done? Across all trials, collagen again looked effective. But trials without funding from companies showed no effect on hydration, elasticity or wrinkles, while company-funded trials did. High-quality trials also showed no significant effect in any category. The authors concluded that there is currently no clinical evidence to support collagen supplements to prevent or treat skin aging.[3]

That pattern — a benefit that appears in the overall pool but vanishes in the strongest and most independent studies — is exactly why the claim "collagen prevents skin aging" is graded D here.

Joints: short-lived and uncertain

For osteoarthritis, a systematic review of 69 trials of dietary supplements found that collagen hydrolysate was among seven supplements with large, clinically important effects on pain in the short term. But the quality of the evidence ranged down to very low, and no supplement showed clinically important pain relief in the long term.[4] Short-term relief in small, low-quality trials is a weak foundation for a long-term habit, which is why lasting benefit is graded D.

Why "eat collagen, make collagen" is not that simple

Skin, tendon and cartilage really are built from related proteins, which is why this site treats them together. But collagen you swallow is broken down in digestion like other dietary protein before the body builds its own tissues. Whether particular fragments do anything special is a reasonable research question; the trials above are the test of it, and the most rigorous ones have not shown a benefit for skin.

The practical bottom line

  • If you want to try collagen, the osteoarthritis review found no safety differences between supplements and placebo in general,[4] but the strongest evidence does not support paying for it to slow skin aging.
  • For painful arthritic joints, a supplement is not a substitute for a proper assessment; non-surgical options will get their own evidence checks here.
  • Be most skeptical of studies funded by the people who sell the product — and of anyone, including doctors, who quotes only the pooled result.
