Does stretching before exercise prevent injuries? Strength training does far more

A meta-analysis of 25 trials with 26,610 people found no injury-prevention effect from stretching, while strength training cut sports injuries to less than a third. A surgeon explains.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 19 October 2026

The verdict

“Stretching before exercise prevents injuries”

D · Unsupported

Key points

  • In a meta-analysis of 25 randomised trials (26,610 people, 3,464 injuries), stretching showed no injury-prevention effect.
  • In the same analysis, strength training reduced sports injuries to less than a third, and proprioception (balance) training also lowered risk.
  • A Cochrane review found stretching before or after exercise made almost no difference to next-day muscle soreness.
  • Stretching does increase flexibility; it just has not been shown to prevent injuries on its own.

Most of us were taught to stretch before sport "so you don't pull something". It is one of the most widely repeated pieces of exercise advice. When researchers put it to the test in randomised trials, the result was surprising.

What the trials found

A systematic review pooled 25 randomised controlled trials covering 26,610 participants and 3,464 injuries, and compared different kinds of preventive exercise.[1]

Type of programme Injury risk vs. no programme
Stretching No clear effect (risk ratio 0.96)
Proprioception / balance training Lower (risk ratio 0.55)
Strength training Much lower (risk ratio 0.32)

The authors summarised it plainly: estimates were favourable for every prevention method except stretching, and strength training reduced sports injuries to less than a third.[1]

What about soreness?

A Cochrane review looked at stretching to prevent delayed-onset muscle soreness. Stretching before exercise changed next-day soreness by about half a point on a 100-point scale, and stretching afterwards by about one point — differences too small for anyone to feel.[2]

The example that works: hamstrings

Hamstring strains are among the most common sports injuries. A meta-analysis of randomised trials found that eccentric hamstring strengthening — exercises where the muscle works while lengthening — reduced hamstring injuries by roughly 57% to 70%. Static stretching improved flexibility, but those gains were more short-lived.[3]

What this means in practice

  • Stretching is fine if you enjoy it or need flexibility for your sport; it just should not be your injury-prevention plan.
  • Regular strength and balance training has much better evidence for preventing injuries.[1]
  • If you have a previous injury, a physiotherapist can tailor a strengthening programme to it.
