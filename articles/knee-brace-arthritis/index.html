Does a knee brace help knee arthritis? What the trials show

Knee braces are one of the most common things people try for an arthritic knee. A surgeon reads a Cochrane review, two meta-analyses of unloader-brace trials that disagree, and the main guidelines to see what a brace can and can't do.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 13 November 2026

The verdict

“A knee brace relieves knee arthritis pain”

C · Early

Evidence at a glance

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • A Cochrane review of 13 studies (1,356 people) found the evidence for knee braces in medial knee arthritis inconclusive; in the one trial with 12-month data (117 people), a brace made no difference to pain compared with no brace.
  • A meta-analysis of 6 randomised trials of unloader (valgus) braces found a small-to-moderate improvement in pain; against a control orthosis, only a small effect remained.
  • A later meta-analysis of 10 randomised trials (739 people) found no significant long-term improvement in pain or function with valgus braces, with possible benefit only in the short term.
  • In the trials, how often people actually wore the brace ranged from 45% to 100%, and up to 25% reported minor complications.
  • Guidelines agree on exercise, education and weight management as core treatment; they differ on braces because the evidence is low quality.

Your knee aches, it's stiff on the stairs, and an X-ray has shown arthritis. One of the first things many people try is a knee brace — a simple sleeve from the pharmacy, or a rigid "unloader" brace designed to shift weight away from the worn side of the knee. Does it actually help?

The honest answer is that the studies disagree. Here is what they found.

What kinds of brace are we talking about?

The trials mostly tested three types:[1]

  • Neoprene sleeves — soft, slip-on supports.
  • Neutral braces — hinged braces that support the knee without changing its alignment.
  • Valgus ("unloader") braces — rigid braces that push the knee slightly outward to take load off the inner (medial) side, where knee arthritis most often starts.

The Cochrane review: inconclusive

A Cochrane review looked at braces and foot or ankle orthoses for knee arthritis, including 13 studies with 1,356 people.[1]

  • Four studies compared a brace with no treatment, but only one (117 people) had usable 12-month data. In that trial, the brace made no difference to pain (mean difference 0.0 on a pain scale), function or quality of life after a year.[1]
  • In that same trial, 24 of 60 people in the brace group stopped because it wasn't helping, compared with 14 of 57 in the no-treatment group.[1]
  • The other brace studies reported some improvement in pain and function.[1]
  • One trial (91 people) compared a valgus brace with a wedged insole: after six months there was no significant difference between them, and both groups improved.[1]

The authors concluded that the evidence for bracing in medial knee arthritis was inconclusive.[1]

Unloader braces: two meta-analyses, two answers

The first pooled 6 randomised trials of valgus braces for medial knee arthritis.[2]

  • Overall, pain improved more with the brace — a small-to-moderate effect (standardised mean difference 0.33).[2]
  • Against no orthosis, the effect on pain was moderate (0.56). Against a control orthosis, only a small effect remained (0.33).[2]
  • Effect sizes varied with how each study was designed.[2]

The second, five years later, pooled 10 randomised trials with 739 people.[3]

  • It found no statistically significant improvement in pain or function with valgus bracing.[3]
  • How long people were followed explained much of the difference between studies; the authors concluded that a valgus brace may help only in short-term rehabilitation, not in the long term.[3]

Put together: an unloader brace may take the edge off pain for a while, but the effect is small, it shrinks when the comparison is a fair one, and there's no good evidence that it lasts. That is why "a knee brace relieves knee arthritis pain" gets a C — the trials don't agree — and "a brace gives lasting relief" gets a D.

Will you actually wear it?

  • In the randomised trials, the share of people who wore the brace as instructed ranged from 45% to 100%, and up to 25% reported minor complications.[2]
  • A 2025 systematic review of bracing for knee arthritis and knee ligament injuries found that skin irritation was the most commonly reported problem.[6]

A rigid brace can be bulky, hot and awkward under clothes. If it isn't worn, it can't help.

What do the guidelines say?

  • The 2019 American College of Rheumatology / Arthritis Foundation guideline made a strong recommendation for tibiofemoral bracing in tibiofemoral knee arthritis, and a conditional one for patellofemoral bracing. It also strongly recommended exercise, weight loss for people who are overweight, self-management programmes, tai chi and using a cane.[4]
  • A 2025 review of 13 international guidelines found they broadly agree on the core: exercise, education and self-management, and weight management. They differ on bracing and orthotics, because guideline groups interpret the generally low level of evidence differently.[5]

So no guideline treats a brace as a replacement for the core treatments. "A brace can replace exercise and weight management" gets a D.

What these studies cannot tell you

  • Most trials were on medial (inner-side) knee arthritis and valgus braces; there is much less evidence for soft sleeves and for arthritis behind the kneecap.[1,2]
  • The trials were small and hard to blind — you know whether you're wearing a brace, which can colour how you rate your pain.[1]
  • No trial shows a brace slows arthritis down; the Cochrane review found long-term effects unknown.[1]
  • Results are averages. Some people find a brace helps on long walks or bad days; the trials can't tell you in advance who those people are.

Verdict

  • "A knee brace relieves knee arthritis pain" — C: one meta-analysis found a small benefit, a larger later one found none long-term, and Cochrane called it inconclusive.[1,2,3]
  • "An unloader brace gives a small short-term improvement in pain" — C.[2,3]
  • "A brace gives lasting relief over a year or more" — D.[1,3]
  • "A brace can replace exercise and weight management" — D.[4,5]
  • "Many people don't wear their brace, and minor problems are common" — B.[2,6]

What this means in practice

  • Start with the treatments every guideline agrees on: regular exercise for the knee, and weight management if you're overweight.[4,5]
  • A brace is a reasonable add-on for some people with medial knee arthritis — for example for long walks — but expect a small effect at best, not a fix.[2,3]
  • If you try one, ask a clinician which type suits your knee, check it fits, and stop if it irritates your skin or you aren't wearing it.[2,6]
  • Get your knee checked if it locks, gives way, swells suddenly or keeps you awake at night — those need an assessment, not just a brace.
