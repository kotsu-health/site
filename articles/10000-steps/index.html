Do you really need 10,000 steps a day? What step-counter studies show

The benefits of walking rise steeply up to about 7,000 steps a day and then level off, especially after 60. A surgeon explains what two large analyses found.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 20 October 2026

The verdict

“You need 10,000 steps a day to get the health benefits of walking”

D · Unsupported

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • The 10,000 figure did not come from research; a 2022 analysis noted there was little evidence for it.
  • In 15 cohorts of 47,471 adults, the risk of death kept falling with more steps up to about 6,000–8,000 a day for people aged 60 and over, and 8,000–10,000 for younger adults.
  • A 2025 review of 57 studies found that, compared with 2,000 steps, 7,000 steps a day was associated with a 47% lower risk of death from any cause.
  • These are observational studies: people who walk more may differ in other ways, so the numbers show association, not proof.

"10,000 steps a day" is printed on fitness trackers, workplace challenges and health posters. It is a neat, round number. When researchers pooled step-counter data from large groups of people, the picture turned out to be more forgiving.

The big pooled analysis

Researchers combined participant-level data from 15 cohorts in several countries: 47,471 adults followed for a median of about 7 years.[1] They noted at the outset that there was little evidence behind the 10,000-step recommendation.[1]

People were split into four groups by their daily steps. Compared with the least active quarter (a median of 3,553 steps), the risk of death was:[1]

Daily steps (group median) Risk of death vs. least active
5,801 40% lower
7,842 45% lower
10,901 53% lower

More steps were better — but not without limit. For adults aged 60 and over, the risk stopped falling at around 6,000–8,000 steps a day; for younger adults, at around 8,000–10,000.[1]

A newer review: beyond survival

A 2025 systematic review of 57 studies looked at many outcomes, not just death. For all-cause mortality, heart disease, dementia and falls, the benefit curve bent at around 5,000–7,000 steps a day.[2]

Compared with 2,000 steps a day, 7,000 steps was associated with a 47% lower risk of death from any cause, 25% lower risk of developing heart disease, 38% lower risk of dementia, 22% lower risk of depressive symptoms and 28% lower risk of falls.[2] The authors concluded that 10,000 can still be a good target for people who are already active, but 7,000 a day is linked to clinically meaningful benefits and may be more realistic for many.[2]

Why this is graded B, not A

These studies counted steps and followed people over time; nobody was assigned to walk more. People who walk more may be healthier in other ways, and the authors themselves flagged limitations such as residual confounding and few studies for some outcomes.[2] The confidence was rated very low for falls and low for some other outcomes.[2]

What this means in practice

  • If 10,000 steps feels out of reach, it is not a pass-or-fail line. Most of the measured benefit appears well before it.
  • Going from very few steps to a few thousand more is where the curves are steepest.[1,2]
  • If knee or hip pain is what keeps your step count low, that is worth getting assessed — it is often treatable.
