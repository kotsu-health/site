Do flat feet cause knee pain?

Flat feet are often blamed for knee pain. A surgeon reads the large Framingham study that measured arches and knees, the systematic reviews of foot posture as an injury risk, and the review of arch-support orthoses for adults with flat feet — and grades the claim.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 28 November 2026

The verdict

“Flat feet cause knee pain”

C · Early

Key points

  • In the Framingham Studies (1,903 adults, mean age 65), the flattest feet had 1.3 times the odds of knee pain on most days and 1.4 times the odds of cartilage damage on the inner side of the knee, compared with other feet.
  • That study looked at one point in time, so it shows a link, not that flat feet cause knee pain.
  • A systematic review of 21 prospective studies (6,228 people) found only very limited evidence, with a small effect, that a flatter (pronated) foot posture predicts pain at the front of the knee.
  • A review of foot orthoses for adults with flat feet found 12 mostly small, low-quality studies and concluded there is a lack of evidence that they help.
  • Flat feet are one possible factor among several; knee pain still needs its own assessment rather than a new pair of insoles by default.

"Your knees hurt because your arches have collapsed" is something many people hear, often followed by a recommendation for arch supports. It sounds mechanical and logical: a flatter foot rolls inward, the shin turns, and the knee takes the strain. Here is what the studies that actually measured feet and knees found.

The biggest study: arches and knees in 1,903 adults

In the Framingham Studies in the US, researchers measured the standing arch of 1,903 adults (56% women, mean age 65) on a pressure mat, asked about knee pain, and read knee MRI scans.[1]

  • 22% of knees were painful on most days.[1]
  • Compared with all other feet, the flattest feet had 1.3 times the odds of knee pain (95% CI 1.1–1.6).[1]
  • They also had 1.4 times the odds of cartilage damage in the inner (medial) part of the knee joint (95% CI 1.1–1.8). The flatter the foot, the higher the odds.[1]
  • There was no link with cartilage damage in the other parts of the knee.[1]

The results were adjusted for age, sex and body mass index.[1] It is a large, carefully measured study, so "flat feet and knee pain go together" is a fair summary, and we grade that link B.

Why a link is not the same as a cause

The Framingham analysis was cross-sectional: arches and knees were measured at the same time.[1] That cannot show which came first. Knee arthritis could change how people stand and walk, or a third factor could affect both. To show that flat feet lead to knee pain, you need studies that measure feet first and follow people to see who develops pain.

Studies that followed people over time

A systematic review gathered 21 prospective studies (6,228 people) that measured foot posture first and then recorded who developed lower-limb overuse injuries.[2]

  • There was very limited evidence that a pronated (flatter, rolled-in) foot posture is a risk factor for patellofemoral pain — pain at the front of the knee — and the effect was small.[2]
  • No link was found between foot posture and foot or ankle injuries, bone stress injuries or general lower-limb overuse injury.[2]
  • The authors advised that foot posture should be only one part of a wider assessment, not the explanation on its own.[2]

A separate review of 39 studies in people with knee osteoarthritis found that a more pronated foot posture was noticed in those with osteoarthritis, but differences in how studies measured the foot made it impossible to establish clearer links.[3]

Do arch supports fix it?

If flat feet were the main cause of knee pain, supporting the arch should help. A systematic review of foot orthoses for adults with flat feet found 12 eligible studies, with 8 to 80 participants each, mostly of low methodological quality, and most measured standing posture and foot pressure rather than pain.[4] The authors concluded that there is a lack of evidence for foot orthoses in adults with flat feet.[4]

That is different from saying insoles never help anyone. We graded a different idea — lateral wedge insoles for knee arthritis — in Do lateral wedge insoles help knee pain from arthritis?, and insoles for heel pain in Do you need custom orthotics for plantar fasciitis?.

Verdict

  • "Flat feet cause knee pain" — C. There is a consistent but modest link in a large study, and only very limited, small-effect evidence from studies that followed people over time.[1,2]
  • "Older adults with the flattest feet report knee pain more often" — B: one large, adjusted cross-sectional study.[1]
  • "A flatter foot posture predicts pain at the front of the knee" — C: very limited evidence of a small effect.[2]
  • "People with knee osteoarthritis tend to have a more pronated foot posture" — C: observed, but the studies are too different to pool.[3]
  • "Arch-support orthoses help adults with flat feet" — C: few, small, low-quality studies.[4]

What this means in practice

  • Having flat feet does not mean your knees are destined to hurt; in the biggest study the difference in odds was modest.[1]
  • If your knee hurts, it deserves its own examination. Flat feet may be one factor, but they are rarely the whole story.[2]
  • For knee arthritis, the treatment with the strongest evidence is exercise; we went through it in Is walking bad for knee arthritis?.
  • A knee that swells, locks or gives way, or pain after an injury, needs a proper assessment.
