Does bad posture cause back pain?

"Sit up straight or you'll ruin your back" — and now "text neck" from phones. A surgeon reads the umbrella review of 41 reviews on posture and back pain, the studies that photographed people's posture, and the trials of what actually prevents back pain.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 24 November 2026

The verdict

“Bad posture causes back pain”

Key points

  • An umbrella review of 41 systematic reviews found both positive and null associations between spine posture, sitting, standing, bending or awkward postures and low back pain, and concluded there is no consensus that these exposures cause it.
  • A meta-analysis of 43 studies found that, on average, people with low back pain did not differ from people without it in the curve of the lower back (lordosis) or in standing pelvic tilt.
  • In 1,108 17-year-olds photographed while sitting, four posture types were identified — from upright to slumped with the head forward — and the odds of neck pain or headache did not differ between them.
  • In 150 young adults, neck posture while texting was not associated with neck pain, whether judged by the participants themselves or by physiotherapists.
  • What has prevented back pain in trials is exercise: in 21 randomised trials with 30,850 people, exercise combined with education lowered the risk of a new episode (relative risk 0.55), while education alone, back belts and shoe insoles did not.

"Sit up straight." "Don't slouch, you'll ruin your back." And now a newer version: "text neck" — the idea that looking down at a phone is wrecking a generation's necks. Posture is probably the most common explanation people give for their own back and neck pain.

It is also one of the most studied. Short version: researchers have looked hard for a link between posture and back pain, and they have not been able to show that posture causes it. What has lowered the risk of back pain in trials is something different: exercise.

Does posture cause low back pain?

  • An umbrella review — a review of the reviews — pulled together 41 systematic reviews on spine posture and physical exposures (prolonged standing, sitting, bending and twisting, awkward postures, heavy physical work) and low back pain. The reviews reported both positive and null associations. Meta-analyses tended to find an association; reviews limited to prospective studies (which follow people over time, and are better at showing what comes first) were less consistent.[1]
  • The authors concluded that, despite the number of reviews, there is no consensus that these physical exposures cause low back pain: association has been documented, but it does not provide a causal explanation, and evidence that they precede a first episode or show a dose-response pattern was mixed.[1]
  • A meta-analysis of 43 studies compared how people with and without low back pain hold and move the lower back. On average, people with back pain showed no difference in the lower-back curve (lordosis) (8 studies) and no difference in standing pelvic tilt (3 studies). They did move their lower back through a smaller range, more slowly, and with poorer joint position sense — but whether those differences come before the pain or because of it is unknown.[2]

So "bad posture causes back pain" gets a C: some studies find associations, but the evidence that posture comes first and causes the pain is not there.[1,2] And the specific belief that people with back pain have a different lower-back curve gets a D — on average, they don't.[2] (For the related rule about bending when you lift, see "Lift with your legs", checked.)

What about slouching and "text neck"?

  • In a study of 1,108 17-year-olds in Australia, researchers photographed each participant sitting and measured the angles of the head, neck and upper back. They found four distinct sitting postures — upright, intermediate, slumped upper back with the head forward, and straight upper back with the head forward. The odds of neck pain or headache did not differ between the four groups. The slumped group did have higher odds of depression, and the upright group exercised more often.[3]
  • In 150 young adults aged 18–21 in Brazil, neck posture while texting was judged both by the participants themselves and by physiotherapists from photographs. There was no association between texting neck posture and neck pain either way (odds ratios 1.66, p = 0.29, and 1.23, p = 0.61), nor with how often neck pain occurred.[4]

Both are cross-sectional studies of young people — a snapshot, not a long follow-up, and not adults with years of desk work behind them.[3,4] Within those limits, "slumped or forward-head sitting causes neck pain" gets a D, and "text neck causes neck pain" a C: one small study found no link, which is not enough to rule it out.[3,4]

Do this instead: what actually lowered the risk of back pain

If posture isn't the lever, what is? A systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 randomised trials with 30,850 participants tested strategies for preventing low back pain.[5]

  • Exercise combined with education reduced the risk of an episode of low back pain — a relative risk of 0.55, moderate-quality evidence. That is roughly a 45% lower risk.[5]
  • Exercise alone may also reduce the risk (relative risk 0.65), although that evidence was low to very low quality.[5]
  • Education alone — being taught about the back, posture and lifting without exercise — had no effect (relative risk 1.03). Neither did back belts (1.01) or shoe insoles (1.01).[5]

So "exercise, with or without education, lowers the risk of an episode of low back pain" gets a B.[5]

What these studies cannot tell you

  • They don't say posture never matters for anyone. The umbrella review found associations in some reviews; it found that causation hasn't been shown.[1]
  • The neck studies were in young people and measured posture once; they can't tell us about decades of working at a screen.[3,4]
  • Changing position may still feel better. Shifting, standing up or moving when a position becomes uncomfortable is a different question from whether a "bad" posture damages the spine — these studies didn't test comfort.

Verdict

  • "Bad posture causes back pain" — C: associations in some reviews, no consensus that it causes pain.[1,2]
  • "People with low back pain have a different lower-back curve" — D.[2]
  • "Slumped or forward-head sitting causes neck pain" — D (one large cross-sectional study, in 17-year-olds).[3]
  • "Text neck causes neck pain" — C (one small study found no link).[4]
  • "Exercise, with or without education, lowers the risk of an episode of low back pain" — B.[5]

What this means in practice

  • You don't need to fear slouching. The research has not shown that sitting or standing "wrong" damages your back or neck.[1,2,3]
  • The comfortable posture is often the next one. If a position starts to ache, change it — that is common sense rather than a tested treatment.
  • Put the effort into exercise. In trials, regular exercise — with or without learning about back pain — was what lowered the risk of a new episode; posture education alone, back belts and insoles did not.[5]
  • Get your back checked if pain spreads down a leg with numbness or weakness, if it follows a fall or injury, if it comes with fever or unexplained weight loss, or if you have new trouble controlling your bladder or bowels — those are not posture questions.
