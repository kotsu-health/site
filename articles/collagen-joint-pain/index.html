Collagen for joints: does it actually help joint pain?

Collagen powders and capsules promise easier, less painful joints. A surgeon reads the meta-analyses of randomised trials in knee osteoarthritis: how much pain relief, for how long, and how collagen compares with other joint supplements.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 22 November 2026

The verdict

“Collagen supplements help joint pain”

C · Early

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • A 2025 meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials (870 people with knee osteoarthritis) found lower pain and better function scores with collagen than with placebo — but the trials disagreed with each other a lot.
  • An earlier meta-analysis found collagen lowered the overall osteoarthritis score and a pain rating, yet the pain part of the main questionnaire did not differ from placebo.
  • A review of 69 supplement trials found short-term pain relief from collagen hydrolysate, but no supplement gave clinically important relief in the long term.
  • Undenatured type II collagen (UC-II) looked promising in 8 small trials (243 people on the supplement), which the authors say need confirming in larger studies.
  • In a 2025 network meta-analysis of 39 trials, collagen helped some outcomes, but Boswellia ranked highest; no supplement increased side effects.

Collagen is the main protein in cartilage, tendons and ligaments, so the idea is intuitive: swallow collagen, help your joints. Powders, gummies and capsules now sell on exactly that promise.

Intuition isn't evidence, though — collagen is digested into small peptides and amino acids like any other protein. What matters is what happened in placebo-controlled trials. Short version: a small, short-term easing of knee arthritis pain in mostly small trials — not lasting relief, and not clearly better than other options.

What the trials found for knee arthritis pain

  • The most recent meta-analysis pooled 11 randomised trials with 870 people with knee osteoarthritis (451 on collagen, 419 on placebo). Collagen groups had lower pain scores (mean difference −13.6 on the pain scales used) and better function scores (−6.5) than placebo.[1]
  • But the trials disagreed with each other a great deal (heterogeneity I² = 88% for pain, 75% for function), which means the size of the benefit varied widely from study to study.[1]
  • An earlier meta-analysis found collagen lowered the overall WOMAC osteoarthritis score (−8.0) and a visual pain rating (−16.6). Yet when the WOMAC was split up, the pain and function sections did not differ from placebo — only stiffness did.[2]

So the signal is there, but it is noisy. That is a C for short-term pain relief: early, inconsistent evidence from mostly small trials.[1,2]

How long does it last?

A systematic review of 69 trials covering 20 different supplements for hand, hip and knee osteoarthritis found collagen hydrolysate among the supplements with large effects on pain in the short term. But the quality of the evidence ranged from high down to very low, and no supplement had clinically important effects on pain in the long term.[3]

"Collagen gives lasting relief from joint pain": D.

Does the type of collagen matter?

Most products are hydrolysed collagen (collagen peptides). A different form, undenatured type II collagen (UC-II), is taken in much smaller doses. A review of 8 randomised trials (243 people on UC-II, follow-up 3–6 months) found better pain and function scores than placebo in early knee osteoarthritis — but the authors describe the literature as limited and call for larger studies.[4] In the 69-trial review, UC-II was one of only two supplements with clinically important pain effects at medium term.[3]

That's promising, not proven: C.

How does collagen compare with other joint supplements?

A 2025 network meta-analysis compared seven supplements across 39 trials (4,599 patients) with knee osteoarthritis. Collagen, curcumin, ginger and krill oil showed benefits in some outcomes, but Boswellia had the highest probability of being most effective for pain and stiffness.[5]

"Collagen is the best joint supplement": D. It is one of several with modest, short-term signals.

Active people and sore joints

A systematic review of 15 randomised trials of collagen peptides combined with exercise — mostly in recreational athletes — concluded that collagen was most beneficial for joint function and joint pain. The studies were small and the authors call for larger, more precise trials.[6] Grade: C.

Is it safe?

In the 69-trial review, supplements as a group did not differ from placebo on safety outcomes (except diacerein, which isn't collagen), and in the 2025 network meta-analysis no supplement was linked to more adverse events than placebo.[3,5] That's reassuring for short-term use: B.

Verdict

  • "Collagen supplements ease knee osteoarthritis pain in the short term" — C: a benefit in pooled trials, but small, inconsistent studies.[1,2,3]
  • "Collagen supplements give lasting relief from joint pain" — D.[3]
  • "Undenatured type II collagen (UC-II) helps early knee osteoarthritis" — C: limited trials.[4]
  • "Collagen is the most effective joint supplement" — D.[5]
  • "Collagen with exercise reduces joint pain in active people" — C.[6]
  • "Collagen supplements are safe to try for joint pain" — B in the short term.[3,5]

Overall: "Collagen supplements help joint pain" — C. It may help a little for a few months; the evidence does not support it as a long-term fix.

What this means in practice

  • If you want to try it, treat it as a short experiment, not a lifelong habit — the trials ran for months, and no supplement showed long-term benefit.[3]
  • Judge it by your own pain and function, and stop if nothing changes; the trials varied too much to predict who benefits.[1]
  • Don't let a supplement replace what does have strong evidence for knee arthritis — exercise, strengthening and, where needed, weight loss (see our articles on walking with knee arthritis and weight loss and knee load).
  • Pain with swelling, locking, giving way, or pain at night deserves an assessment rather than another supplement.
