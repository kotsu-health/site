Do noisy, cracking knees mean arthritis is coming?

Grinding, cracking or popping when you squat or climb stairs worries a lot of people. A surgeon reads the 2025 meta-analysis of 103 studies on knee crepitus and two long-term US cohorts to see what the noise does, and does not, predict.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 8 November 2026

The verdict

“A noisy, cracking knee means arthritis is coming”

C · Early

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • Knee crepitus — grinding, cracking or popping during movement — is common: a 2025 meta-analysis of 103 studies estimated it in 41% of the general population and 36% of people with no knee pain.
  • Noisy knees are linked with osteoarthritis: crepitus was reported in 81% of knees with osteoarthritis and was associated with about 3.8 times the odds of osteoarthritis on X-ray — but the certainty of this evidence was rated low to very low.
  • In a US cohort of 3,495 people without symptomatic knee arthritis, those who reported crepitus more often were more likely to develop painful arthritis over four years; most of these new cases were in knees that already had arthritis changes on X-ray.
  • In the same US cohort, crepitus did not predict who had a knee replacement over the next three years.
  • A noisy knee without pain, swelling or giving way is not a diagnosis; noise together with persistent pain or swelling is worth having assessed.

You squat, climb a step or stand up from a chair, and your knee grinds, crackles or pops. Clinicians call this crepitus, and it is one of the most common reasons people start to worry that arthritis is on its way. The noise itself has now been studied in tens of thousands of knees. The answer is more nuanced than either "it's nothing" or "it's arthritis".

How common is a noisy knee?

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in the British Journal of Sports Medicine pooled 103 studies with 36,439 participants (42,816 knees).[1]

  • In the general population, about 41% of knees had crepitus.[1]
  • In people with no knee pain, the estimate was about 36%.[1]
  • In knees with osteoarthritis, it was about 81%.[1]
  • Across other knee conditions it ranged from about 35% (ligament injury) to 61% (cartilage problems).[1]

So a noisy knee is very common — including in many people whose knees do not hurt. The authors rated the certainty of these pooled estimates as very low, mainly because the studies measured crepitus in different ways and their results varied widely.[1]

Is the noise linked to arthritis?

Yes, on average. In the same review, knee crepitus was associated with about 3.8 times the odds of osteoarthritis on X-ray (odds ratio 3.79, 1,725 knees), and with several osteoarthritis-related features on MRI.[1] The certainty of that link was rated low to very low, and the authors advise interpreting it with caution.[1]

An association in a snapshot does not tell you which came first. A knee that already has early arthritis may simply be noisier.

Does a noisy knee predict arthritis later?

The best long-term data come from the US Osteoarthritis Initiative, which followed people at higher risk of knee arthritis with yearly questionnaires and X-rays.

  • In 3,495 people (average age 61) whose knees did not have painful arthritis at the start, the odds of developing painful, X-ray-confirmed arthritis over four years rose with how often they reported crepitus: about 1.5 times for "rarely", 1.8 for "sometimes", 2.2 for "often" and 3.0 for "always", compared with people who never noticed it.[2]
  • But the group whose knees already showed arthritis on X-ray, without frequent pain, contributed 26% of the observations and more than 75% of the new cases.[2] In other words, much of the "prediction" came from knees in which arthritis had, in effect, already started.
  • The study did not systematically assess the kneecap joint (patellofemoral joint), a common source of crepitus.[2]

In another analysis of the same cohort (4,566 participants), crepitus at the start did not predict having a knee replacement over the following three years.[3] People with crepitus did lose slightly more knee-straightening strength over four years — a difference of about 2% — which did not appear to affect their physical function or quality of life.[3]

What these studies cannot tell you

  • They are observational. They show who tended to develop arthritis, not that the noise causes it, and they cannot predict any one person's knee.[2,3]
  • Most measured crepitus by asking people. How often you notice a sound is not a precise measurement of what is happening inside the joint.[1,2]
  • The cohort was at higher risk to begin with. Results may not apply in the same way to younger, pain-free people.[2]

Verdict

  • "A noisy, cracking knee means arthritis is coming" — C. Crepitus is linked with arthritis and with a higher chance of developing painful arthritis, but it is common in pain-free knees, the certainty is low, and most new cases in the main cohort were in knees already showing X-ray changes.[1,2]
  • "Knee crepitus is common, even in pain-free knees" — C: about 36–41% in pooled data, very low certainty.[1]
  • "Crepitus is associated with arthritis on X-ray and MRI" — C: odds about 3.8 times higher, low to very low certainty.[1]
  • "More frequent crepitus goes with a higher risk of painful arthritis" — B: a consistent dose–response in a large cohort, but observational.[2]
  • "Crepitus predicts needing a knee replacement within a few years" — D: it did not, in more than 4,500 people followed for three years.[3]

What this means in practice

  • A knee that is noisy but not painful, swollen or unstable is common and, on its own, is not a diagnosis of arthritis.[1]
  • Because frequent crepitus is linked with a higher chance of later painful arthritis, especially in knees already showing changes, it is a reasonable reminder to look after the knee rather than a reason for alarm.[2]
  • Noise together with pain that keeps coming back, swelling, locking or giving way is different — that combination is worth an in-person assessment.
