Does a knee replacement only last 10–15 years?

Many people put off a knee replacement because they have heard it wears out after 10 or 15 years. A surgeon reads the Lancet registry meta-analysis of nearly 300,000 knees and a UK study of 54,000 patients on how long the implants last, and why age at surgery matters.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 3 November 2026

The verdict

“A knee replacement only lasts 10–15 years”

D · Unsupported

Evidence at a glance

ClaimGradeRefs
A knee replacement only lasts 10–15 yearsD · Unsupported1, 2
Most total knee replacements are still in place, without revision, 20–25 years laterB · Moderate1, 2
Younger patients have a higher lifetime risk of needing a revisionB · Moderate2

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • A Lancet meta-analysis of national joint registries (299,291 total knee replacements) found that about 82% were still in place, without revision, 25 years after surgery.
  • A UK study of 54,276 people found 10-year implant survival of 96.1% and 20-year survival of 89.7% for total knee replacement.
  • Age at surgery changes the picture: for people over 70 the lifetime risk of revision after hip or knee replacement was about 5%, but it rose to as much as 35% for men in their early 50s.
  • 'Survival' in these studies means the implant was not replaced; it does not measure pain or how well the knee works.
  • Today's implants are newer than most of those followed for 20–25 years, so these figures describe the past and cannot promise any one person's result.

"Wait as long as you can — a new knee only lasts 10 or 15 years." It is one of the most common reasons people give for putting off a knee replacement. How long implants last has been measured, in very large numbers.

What the registries show

Many countries record every joint replacement in a national registry and follow each implant until it is revised (taken out or replaced) or the person dies. A systematic review in The Lancet pooled these registry reports: 299,291 total knee replacements from 47 series.[1] Using data from 14 registries, an estimated 82.3% of total knee replacements were still in place 25 years after surgery.[1] For partial (unicondylar) knee replacements, the 25-year figure was lower, 69.8%, from four registries.[1] The authors considered the registry data more accurate than the smaller published case series they also reviewed.[1]

A separate UK population study followed 54,276 people who had a total knee replacement between 1991 and 2011 for up to 20 years.[2] Implant survival was 96.1% at 10 years and 89.7% at 20 years.[2]

So on average, a total knee replacement lasting only 10–15 years is not what these large datasets show.

Why age at surgery matters

The same UK study turned survival into a lifetime risk of revision, taking into account how long people of each age are likely to live.[2]

  • For people over 70 at the time of hip or knee replacement, the lifetime risk of a revision was about 5%, with no difference between men and women.[2]
  • Below 70, the risk rose the younger people were: up to 35% for men in their early 50s, and about 15 percentage points lower for women of the same age.[2]
  • In people who had surgery before 60, the median time to revision, among those revised, was 4.4 years.[2]

A younger, more active person simply has more years — and more use — in which something can go wrong. That is the part of the "it won't last" worry that the evidence does support.

What these numbers cannot tell you

  • "Survival" is not the same as a good knee. It counts only whether the implant was replaced. A knee that is still painful or stiff but not revised counts as a success.[1]
  • They describe older implants. Knees followed for 20–25 years were put in decades ago; current designs and techniques have not yet been followed that long, so their long-term results are not known.
  • They are averages. The authors note that more information is needed to predict how long a specific knee replacement will last.[1] These are observational data, so they show what happened, not what will happen for one person — which is why the positive claims are graded B.

Verdict

  • "A knee replacement only lasts 10–15 years" — D. In registry data, about 82% of total knee replacements were still in place at 25 years, and about 90% at 20 years in a UK population study.[1,2]
  • "Most total knee replacements last 20–25 years" — B: consistent, very large observational datasets, but on older implants.[1,2]
  • "Younger patients are more likely to need a revision" — B: lifetime revision risk about 5% over 70, up to 35% for men in their early 50s.[2]

What this means in practice

  • The fear that a new knee will need replacing within 10–15 years should not, on its own, be the reason to delay surgery when arthritis is severe; on average implants last much longer.[1,2]
  • Age is a real factor in the decision: the younger you are, the higher the chance of a second operation over your lifetime.[2] That trade-off is worth talking through with your surgeon.
  • These studies looked at knee replacements done for end-stage osteoarthritis.[1] Whether and when surgery makes sense depends on your symptoms, your X-rays and what other treatment has achieved — a decision for an in-person assessment, not a number.
