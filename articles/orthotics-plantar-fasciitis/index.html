Do you need custom orthotics for plantar fasciitis?

Custom-made insoles are one of the most common things people are told to try for heel pain. A surgeon reads three meta-analyses of the trials — custom vs off-the-shelf vs sham insoles — and a strength-training trial to see what actually helps.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 18 November 2026

The verdict

“You need custom orthotics for plantar fasciitis”

D · Unsupported

Key points

  • A meta-analysis of 20 trials found no difference in short-term pain between custom and prefabricated (off-the-shelf) foot orthoses — and neither type beat sham insoles.
  • A second meta-analysis of 19 trials (1,660 people) found a small benefit of orthoses over sham insoles at 7–12 weeks, of uncertain clinical importance, and no difference between custom and prefabricated orthoses at any time point.
  • A network meta-analysis of 31 trials (2,450 people) found no common treatment for plantar heel pain clearly better than the others.
  • In one small trial (48 people), heel raises with a towel under the toes every second day gave better foot function at 3 months than stretching, but no difference at 6 or 12 months.

The first steps out of bed are the worst: a sharp pain under the heel that eases a little once you get moving. That's the classic pattern of plantar heel pain, often called plantar fasciitis. One of the first things many people are told is to get custom orthotics — insoles moulded to their own feet. Are they worth it?

Three meta-analyses have pooled the trials. Here is what they found.

Custom vs off-the-shelf vs sham insoles

The first meta-analysis included 20 trials testing eight different types of foot orthoses for plantar heel pain; most of the studies were of high quality.[1]

  • Off-the-shelf vs sham: pooled data from six studies showed no difference in short-term pain between prefabricated orthoses and sham (fake) insoles.[1]
  • Custom vs sham: no difference in short-term pain.[1]
  • Custom vs off-the-shelf: no difference in short-term pain (mean difference 0.03 on the pain scale).[1]
  • The authors concluded that foot orthoses were not superior to sham insoles or to other conservative treatments for pain and function.[1]

The second meta-analysis included 19 trials with 1,660 people.[2]

  • At 7–12 weeks, there was moderate-quality evidence that orthoses reduced pain more than sham insoles — a small effect (standardised mean difference −0.27), and the authors were unsure whether it was large enough to matter to patients.[2]
  • In the short term (up to 6 weeks) and the longer term (13–52 weeks), only very low-quality evidence was available, and it suggested no benefit.[2]
  • Custom vs prefabricated: no difference at any time point.[2]

Put together: two independent groups, reading largely the same trials, both found that custom orthotics did no better than off-the-shelf ones. That's why "custom orthotics work better than off-the-shelf insoles" gets a D. Whether any insole helps beyond a sham is less clear — at most a small effect in the medium term — so "shoe inserts relieve plantar fasciitis pain" gets a C.

Is any treatment clearly the best?

A network meta-analysis compared the common treatments — steroid injection, anti-inflammatory tablets, exercise, orthoses and shockwave therapy — across 31 trials with 2,450 people.[3]

  • The available evidence did not show that any of these treatments was better than any other; data were sparse and confidence intervals wide.[3]
  • Steroid injection (alone or with exercise) and shockwave therapy were ranked most likely to help, and placebo/sham least likely — but a ranking is not proof of a real difference.[3]
  • Exercise appeared to help only in the long term.[3]

So "one treatment is clearly the best for heel pain" gets a D: the trials can't pick a winner.

Strength training: a small trial worth knowing about

A Danish trial randomised 48 people with ultrasound-confirmed plantar fasciitis. Everyone got shoe inserts; one group also did daily plantar-fascia stretching, the other did high-load strength training every second day — single-leg heel raises with a towel rolled under the toes.[4]

  • At 3 months, the strength group's foot function score was 29 points better (on the Foot Function Index) than the stretching group's.[4]
  • At 1, 6 and 12 months, there was no difference between the groups.[4]

That suggests heel-raise training may help people get better sooner, not that it changes where they end up a year later. With only 48 people in one trial, "heel-raise strength training speeds early recovery compared with stretching" gets a C.

What these studies cannot tell you

  • Most trials were small and short. Long-term data on insoles are of very low quality.[2]
  • "Sham" insoles are not nothing. A flat insole still cushions the heel, which may be why real and sham insoles performed so similarly.
  • Averages hide individuals. Some people do feel better in a particular insole; the trials can't say in advance who.
  • Not all heel pain is plantar fasciitis. The trials studied plantar heel pain; numbness, night pain, pain after a fall or heel pain with swelling and fever are different problems.

Verdict

  • "Custom orthotics work better than off-the-shelf insoles" — D: no difference in two meta-analyses.[1,2]
  • "Shoe inserts relieve plantar fasciitis pain" — C: no better than sham in one meta-analysis, a small medium-term effect of uncertain importance in the other.[1,2]
  • "One treatment is clearly the best for heel pain" — D.[3]
  • "Heel-raise strength training speeds early recovery compared with stretching" — C: one small trial, benefit at 3 months only.[4]

What this means in practice

  • If you want to try an insole, the trials give no reason to start with a custom-made one — off-the-shelf orthoses did just as well.[1,2]
  • Don't expect an insole alone to fix it; at best the effect was small.[1,2]
  • Heel raises (slowly, with a towel under the toes, every second day) are a reasonable thing to discuss with a physiotherapist or doctor — they may speed early improvement.[4]
  • Get your heel checked if the pain followed a fall or sudden "pop", if your foot is numb or tingling, if it hurts at night or at rest, or if it isn't improving after a few months.
