Do cold plunges speed muscle recovery? What the meta-analyses show — and the catch for lifters

Ice baths are everywhere. A surgeon reads the meta-analyses: cold water eases soreness, does little for strength recovery, and done after every strength session may shrink your gains.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 26 October 2026

The verdict

“Cold plunges speed up muscle recovery”

C · Early

Key points

  • A Cochrane review of 17 small trials found less muscle soreness 24 to 96 hours after exercise with cold-water immersion than with rest, but study quality was low.
  • In 52 studies, cold water helped muscular power and soreness 24 hours after hard exercise, but did not change the recovery of strength.
  • An umbrella review of 15 meta-analyses rated the certainty of the evidence as low to very low.
  • In a 12-week trial, men who took a 10-minute cold bath after each strength session gained less strength and muscle than men who did an active cool-down.
  • If your goal is bigger or stronger muscles, the evidence suggests skipping the cold plunge right after strength training.

Cold plunges and ice baths have gone from elite locker rooms to back gardens. The claim behind them is simple: cold water speeds up muscle recovery. "Recovery" can mean several things, though — feeling less sore, getting your power back, or getting stronger over months. The evidence treats them very differently.

Soreness: a real but modest effect

A Cochrane review pooled 17 small trials with 366 participants.[1] Compared with rest, cold-water immersion reduced muscle soreness at 24, 48, 72 and 96 hours after exercise.[1] The authors rated study quality as low, found the results heterogeneous, and noted that most trials did not actively look for side effects.[1]

A later meta-analysis of 52 studies reached a similar answer for soreness after high-intensity exercise, and also found lower levels of a muscle-damage marker (creatine kinase) and better perceived recovery at 24 hours.[2] A review restricted to 9 randomised trials found cold water only "slightly better" than passive recovery for soreness, with the best results at about 11–15 °C for 11–15 minutes.[3]

Performance: power yes, strength no

In the same 52-study meta-analysis, cold water improved the recovery of muscular power (such as jumping) 24 hours after hard exercise — but it had no significant effect on the recovery of strength.[2] A 2026 umbrella review of 15 meta-analyses (161 studies) agreed: power and jump performance recovered better at 24–48 hours, strength did not, and jump performance was actually worse in the first hours after the plunge.[4] It rated the overall certainty of evidence as low to very low.[4]

The catch: smaller gains from strength training

Feeling recovered is not the same as adapting. In a 12-week randomised trial, 21 active men did strength training twice a week, followed by either 10 minutes of cold-water immersion or an active cool-down.[5] Strength and muscle mass increased more in the active cool-down group.[5] Type II muscle fibre size rose by 17% with the active cool-down, but not with cold water.[5] A biopsy study by the same team found that cold water blunted the signals that drive muscle growth.[5]

The umbrella review also concluded that cold-water immersion impaired gains in strength and muscle size, and recommended using it selectively rather than after every session.[4] The key trial was small and in young men, so how much this applies to older adults or casual exercisers is uncertain.

Verdict

Cold water reliably makes muscles feel less sore and helps power bounce back a day later, but it does not speed the recovery of strength, and the evidence is of low certainty overall.[1,2,4] The broad claim that cold plunges "speed up muscle recovery" is graded C — partly true, overstated. Used after every strength session, they may cost you some of the muscle and strength you trained for.[4,5]

What this means in practice

  • If soreness is stopping you from training, or you compete again within a day or two, a cold plunge is a reasonable option.[2,4]
  • If your goal is more muscle or strength, the evidence suggests avoiding cold immersion straight after strength training.[4,5]
  • Protocols that worked best in the trials were about 10–15 minutes in water around 10–15 °C — colder and longer was not clearly better.[3,4]
  • Sudden immersion in cold water is a shock to the body. If you have a heart condition, high blood pressure or a circulation problem, check with your doctor first.
