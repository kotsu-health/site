Does bending your back when you lift cause back pain? 'Lift with your legs', checked

Almost everyone has been told to keep their back straight and lift with their legs. A surgeon reads a meta-analysis of lifting posture and back pain, a Cochrane review of lifting-technique training in more than 20,000 workers, and a meta-analysis of what actually prevents back pain.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 11 November 2026

The verdict

“Learning to lift with your legs and a straight back prevents back pain”

D · Unsupported

Evidence at a glance

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • A meta-analysis of lifting studies found low-quality evidence that bending the lower back more when lifting was not a risk factor for back pain, and did not distinguish people with and without back pain; 9 of 11 studies found no significant difference.
  • In the one longitudinal study and three cross-sectional studies that measured the lumbar spine directly, peak bending during lifting did not differ between people with and without back pain.
  • A Cochrane review of randomised trials found moderate-quality evidence that training in lifting technique, with or without lifting aids, did not prevent back pain or back-pain disability in workers (7 trials, 19,317 employees).
  • A meta-analysis of 21 randomised trials found that exercise combined with education reduced the risk of an episode of low back pain (RR 0.55); education alone did not (RR 1.03), and back belts did not either.
  • Lifting posture does change the forces on the spine in biomechanical studies, and heavy, repetitive lifting at work was linked with more back pain in field studies — so load and fatigue still matter.

"Bend your knees, keep your back straight, lift with your legs." It's on workplace posters, in safety courses and in most people's heads. The idea is simple: a rounded back when you lift is what hurts backs. But has anyone actually checked whether lifting this way prevents back pain?

Do people with back pain bend their backs more when they lift?

A systematic review with meta-analysis looked for studies that measured how much people bent their lower back (lumbar flexion) while lifting, and asked two questions: does more bending predict back pain, and do people with back pain lift differently?[1]

  • Four studies (one longitudinal, three cross-sectional) measured the lumbar spine itself. Peak bending during lifting did not differ between people with and without back pain.[1]
  • Seven cross-sectional studies used a cruder measure (the angle between chest and pelvis). In these, people with back pain bent their lower back about 6 degrees less than people without it — the opposite of what the poster would predict.[1]
  • Nine of the 11 studies found no significant difference in lumbar bending between the groups.[1]

The authors concluded there was low-quality evidence that greater lumbar flexion during lifting was not a risk factor for back pain, and did not tell people with and without back pain apart.[1] The included studies were of low quality, and only one followed people over time — so this does not prove a rounded back is harmless. It means the evidence that it causes back pain isn't there. That is why "lifting with a rounded back causes back pain" gets a C, not a D.

Does teaching people to "lift properly" prevent back pain?

This has been tested directly in randomised trials, mostly in workers who handle loads for a living. A Cochrane review included nine randomised trials (20,101 employees) and nine cohort studies.[2]

  • Training was compared with no intervention, professional education, a video, a back belt or exercise. Its intensity ranged from a single session to extensive personal biofeedback.[2]
  • None of the included studies showed a preventive effect of training on back pain.[2]
  • In seven trials (19,317 employees), people who received training reported back pain at levels similar to those who received no intervention (odds ratio 1.17, 95% CI 0.68 to 2.02) or minor advice by video (relative risk 0.93, 95% CI 0.69 to 1.25).[2]
  • The authors rated this moderate-quality evidence that lifting advice and training, with or without assistive devices, does not prevent back pain or back-pain disability.[2]

So the claim "learning to lift with your legs prevents back pain" gets a D.

Does lifting posture matter at all?

Yes — for the forces on the spine, if not clearly for pain. A 2026 systematic review of ten biomechanical and field studies in workers found that:[4]

  • Squat lifting (bending the knees) reduced shear forces at the lower spine but increased overall muscular effort, while stoop lifting produced higher shear load at L5/S1.[4]
  • People with back pain showed altered movement strategies, such as less lumbar flexion and more movement at the pelvis.[4]
  • In field studies, repetitive manual handling and constrained postures were linked with more back pain; asymmetry, low strength and fatigue also contributed.[4]

These are mostly laboratory and cross-sectional findings, so they show how load is distributed, not that one technique prevents pain.[4] Heavy, repeated lifting while tired is a different question from whether your back is a few degrees rounded.

What does prevent back pain?

A meta-analysis of 21 randomised trials (30,850 participants) compared prevention strategies head to head:[3]

  • Exercise combined with education reduced the risk of an episode of low back pain (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.41 to 0.74; moderate-quality evidence).[3]
  • Exercise alone may also reduce it (RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.86; low- to very-low-quality evidence).[3]
  • Education alone showed no effect (RR 1.03), and neither did back belts (RR 1.01) or shoe insoles (RR 1.01).[3]

What these studies cannot tell you

  • The posture evidence is low quality and mostly cross-sectional; it can't show what causes what.[1]
  • The training trials were in workplaces, and several had a high risk of bias; confidence intervals were wide.[2]
  • None of this covers acute injury from a single very heavy or awkward lift, or people with a known spinal condition who have been given specific advice.
  • Biomechanical load is not the same as pain. Posture changes spinal forces, but the link from those forces to back pain was not shown in these reviews.[1,4]

Verdict

  • "Learning to lift with your legs and a straight back prevents back pain" — D: moderate-quality evidence from randomised trials in more than 19,000 workers found no preventive effect.[2]
  • "Lifting with a rounded lower back is a risk factor for back pain" — C: low-quality evidence points against it, but it isn't strong enough to rule it out.[1]
  • "Exercise lowers the risk of an episode of back pain" — B: a meta-analysis of randomised trials, strongest when combined with education.[3]
  • "Back belts prevent back pain" — D: no effect in trials.[2,3]
  • "Lifting posture changes the loads on the spine" — C: biomechanical and field studies, with trade-offs between techniques.[4]

What this means in practice

  • There is no good evidence that a rounded back when you pick something up is, by itself, what causes back pain — so you don't need to fear every bend.[1]
  • What has the best evidence for preventing back pain is regular exercise, ideally with education about back pain.[3]
  • Heavy, repeated lifting and fatigue were still linked with more back pain in field studies, so how much and how often you lift matters even if posture rules alone don't prevent pain.[4]
  • If back pain comes with leg weakness or numbness, problems controlling your bladder or bowels, fever, or follows a fall or injury, get assessed promptly.
