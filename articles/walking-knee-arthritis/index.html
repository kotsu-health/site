Is walking bad for knee arthritis? What the studies show

Many people with arthritic knees cut back on walking to 'save' the joint. A surgeon reads a US cohort of 1,212 people with knee arthritis, a step-count study of 1,788 people and two reviews of exercise trials to see whether walking wears the knee out.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 9 November 2026

The verdict

“Walking wears out a knee that already has arthritis”

D · Unsupported

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • In a US cohort of 1,212 people aged 50 or older with knee arthritis, those who walked for exercise had about 0.6 times the odds of developing new frequent knee pain over four years compared with non-walkers.
  • In the same cohort, worsening of joint-space narrowing on X-ray was slightly less common in walkers, though this result was borderline.
  • In 1,788 people with or at risk of knee arthritis, each extra 1,000 steps a day was linked with a 16–18% lower risk of developing functional limitation over two years.
  • Across randomised trials, land-based exercise reduced knee arthritis pain by about 12 points on a 0–100 scale (high-quality evidence); no trial reported a serious adverse event.
  • These walking studies are observational, so they show an association, not proof; pain that keeps rising, or a hot, swollen knee, is a reason to get the knee assessed.

When a knee starts to ache with arthritis, a common instinct is to walk less — as if the joint had a fixed number of steps left and every walk used some of them up. It is an understandable idea. It is also one that has been tested, in people who already have knee arthritis.

Do walkers with knee arthritis do worse?

The clearest data come from the US Osteoarthritis Initiative. Researchers looked at 1,212 people aged 50 or older who already had knee arthritis (average age 63) and asked whether they had walked for exercise since age 50.[1] About 73% said they had.[1]

Over four years:

  • Walkers had about 0.6 times the odds of developing new frequent knee pain compared with non-walkers (odds ratio 0.6, 95% CI 0.4–0.8).[1]
  • Narrowing of the inner (medial) joint space on X-ray — a sign of cartilage loss — progressed slightly less often in walkers (odds ratio 0.8, 95% CI 0.6–1.0), a borderline result.[1]

The authors concluded that walking for exercise should be encouraged for people with knee arthritis, and that whether it could actually slow the disease needs further study.[1]

What about how much you walk?

In the Multicenter Osteoarthritis Study, 1,788 people with or at risk of knee arthritis (average age 67) wore a step counter for a week.[2]

  • Each additional 1,000 steps a day was associated with a 16% to 18% lower risk of developing functional limitation over two years — measured both as slow walking speed and by questionnaire.[2]
  • Walking fewer than about 6,000 steps a day best separated those who went on to develop functional limitation from those who did not.[2] The authors call this a preliminary estimate, not a target proven in a trial.[2]

What do the trials say?

Walking studies like these are observational. For cause and effect, we need randomised trials of exercise.

  • A Cochrane review of land-based exercise for knee arthritis found high-quality evidence from 44 trials (3,537 participants) that exercise reduced pain by about 12 points on a 0–100 scale immediately after treatment, with a smaller benefit of about 6 points still present two to six months later.[3]
  • In that review, eight studies reported adverse events — all were increased knee or low back pain — and no study reported a serious adverse event.[3]
  • A 2025 meta-analysis of 24 studies (1,972 participants) of low-intensity activity in middle-aged and older adults with knee arthritis — walking, tai chi, Baduanjin and general physical activity — found a moderate reduction in pain, and each of these activities, walking included, reduced pain.[4]

What these studies cannot tell you

  • The walking studies are observational. People who walk may differ in other ways from people who don't, so the link is not proof that walking itself protected their knees.[1,2]
  • Walking was recalled, not measured, in the main cohort. The walking questionnaire was given years after the start of the study.[1]
  • The trials tested many kinds of exercise. The Cochrane estimate is for land-based exercise in general, not walking alone.[3]

Verdict

  • "Walking wears out a knee that already has arthritis" — D. In people who already had knee arthritis, walkers were less likely to develop frequent knee pain, and their X-rays did not worsen faster.[1,2]
  • "People with knee arthritis who walk for exercise develop less frequent knee pain" — B: a consistent finding in a large cohort, but observational.[1]
  • "More daily steps go with less risk of functional limitation" — B: a dose–response link in a large cohort, but observational.[2]
  • "Exercise reduces knee arthritis pain in the short term" — A: high-quality evidence from 44 randomised trials.[3]
  • "Low-intensity activity such as walking or tai chi reduces knee arthritis pain" — B: a meta-analysis of 24 studies; walking was one of several activities included.[4]

What this means in practice

  • Arthritis is not a reason, on its own, to stop walking: in the studies we have, walkers with knee arthritis did no worse, and on average did better.[1,2]
  • Exercise programmes can make the knee sore at times; in the trials, that was the only kind of adverse event reported.[3]
  • Pain that keeps rising week after week, a hot or swollen knee, locking or giving way is different — that is worth an in-person assessment.
