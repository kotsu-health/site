Biotin for hair: a popular supplement with a hidden lab-test problem

Biotin has helped hair only in people with a deficiency or an underlying condition. In everyone else the evidence is missing — and high doses can distort important blood tests.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 15 October 2026

The verdict

“Biotin grows hair in healthy people”

Key points

  • A review of published cases found that every person whose hair improved on biotin had an underlying condition causing poor growth.
  • There is no good evidence that biotin grows hair in healthy people.
  • High blood levels of biotin can interfere with common lab tests, including thyroid tests, and cause diagnostic errors.
  • If you take biotin, tell whoever orders your blood tests.

This is a Trend Check: an evidence review outside the author's surgical specialty. It does not recommend any product or treatment. For hair loss, see a dermatologist.

Biotin — vitamin B7 — is one of the best-selling "hair, skin and nails" supplements. The marketing is simple: biotin is involved in making keratin, so more biotin means more hair. Here is what the research actually shows.

Who biotin actually helps

A review of the published reports found 18 cases in which biotin was used for hair or nail problems. In every one of them, the person had an underlying condition causing poor hair or nail growth, and all improved. The authors concluded that, despite its popularity, there is not enough evidence for biotin supplementation in healthy people.[1]

A broader review of vitamins and minerals in hair loss came to a similar place: deficiencies may matter, but large placebo-controlled trials are needed to show whether supplements help hair growth, particularly in people who are actually deficient.[2]

So the honest summary is: biotin can help the uncommon person who lacks it or has certain conditions (grade C, based on case reports), but there is no good evidence that it grows hair in everyone else (grade D).

The problem most people don't know about

Many blood tests use biotin as part of their chemistry. When biotin levels in the blood are high, as they can be after taking supplements, it can push results falsely up or falsely down depending on how the test is designed. A review of the problem notes that this can cause diagnostic errors, most commonly in thyroid testing, where it can make results look like an overactive thyroid.[3]

What to do with this

  • If you are losing hair, the useful step is finding the cause, which a dermatologist can help with — not a supplement chosen at random.
  • If you take biotin, tell the doctor or clinic before any blood test, because it may affect the result.
  • Be wary of "hair, skin and nails" products that rely on biotin's role in the body rather than on trials in people like you.
