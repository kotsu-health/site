Is it too late to build muscle after 60? What the trials show

Many people believe lost muscle can't be rebuilt in later life. A surgeon reads the meta-analyses of strength training in adults over 50, over 75 and even over 90.

By John Ishiguro, MD, Board-certified orthopedic surgeon, Tokyo · 25 October 2026

The verdict

“It is too late to build muscle and strength in later life”

D · Unsupported

Grades follow our method. A strong link between two things is not proof that one causes the other.

Key points

  • In 47 studies of adults aged 50 and over, strength training raised strength by about 24–33% depending on the exercise.
  • In 49 studies (1,328 people aged 50 and over), strength training added an average of 1.1 kg of lean body mass.
  • A meta-analysis of 22 randomised trials in people aged 75 and over found gains in both muscle strength and muscle size, including in those aged 80 and over.
  • Gains get smaller with age, so starting earlier helps — but they do not disappear.
  • Serious adverse events were rare in the trials; minor muscle and joint aches were common.

Muscle mass and strength decline with age, and many people assume that once it has gone, it has gone for good. That belief is easy to test: researchers have put older adults on strength-training programmes and measured what happened.

Strength: large gains after 50

A meta-analysis pooled 47 studies with 1,079 adults aged 50 and over.[2] Strength training improved every strength test measured:[2]

Exercise Average gain
Leg press 29%
Chest press 24%
Knee extension 33%
Lat pull-down 25%

Higher-intensity training was associated with greater improvement.[2] A Cochrane review of 121 randomised trials with 6,700 older people also found a large effect of progressive strength training on muscle strength, and improvements in some everyday tasks such as getting up from a chair.[6]

Muscle mass: it does grow

A second meta-analysis of 49 studies (1,328 people aged 50 and over) looked at lean body mass. On average, strength training added 1.1 kg (95% CI 0.9 to 1.2 kg).[1] Higher-volume programmes were associated with bigger gains.[1]

What about 75, 80 or 90?

A 2020 meta-analysis included only randomised trials in people aged 75 and over — 22 studies.[3] Strength training increased muscle strength, including in a subgroup aged 80 and over, and increased whole-muscle size.[3] There were few reports of adverse events.[3]

One small, often-cited study went further. Ten frail nursing-home residents aged about 90 did 8 weeks of high-intensity leg training. In the 9 who finished, strength rose by an average of 174%, thigh muscle area by 9% and walking speed in a tandem-gait test by 48%.[4] It had no control group and only 9 completers, so it shows what is possible rather than what to expect.

The honest catch: gains shrink with age

Older age does blunt the response. In the lean-mass meta-analysis, older participants gained less.[1] A meta-analysis of 35 studies of muscle fibres also found that fibres grew with training, but less as age increased.[5] The authors of the lean-mass analysis suggested that starting earlier in life may be more effective.[1] Many of the included studies were small, and some were not randomised, so the exact size of the gains is uncertain.

Verdict

The claim that it is too late to build muscle and strength in later life is contradicted by meta-analyses in adults over 50 and in randomised trials of people over 75 and 80.[1,2,3] Graded D. The gains are smaller than in younger people, but they are real.[1,5]

What this means in practice

  • Strength gains appear at any age studied, so there is no age at which training stops being worthwhile.[2,3]
  • Progression matters: the programmes that worked increased the load or volume over time.[1,2]
  • Expect some muscle soreness or joint aches at first; serious problems were rare in the trials.[3,6]
  • If you have a heart condition, recent surgery, or pain that stops you exercising, check with your doctor or a physiotherapist first.
